Positive Geometries for Barycentric Interpolation

Márton Vaitkus, Budapest University of Technology and Economics

Barycentric coordinates are ratios of functions that become infinite on the boundary. Positive geometries say where these functions come from.

→ Space next · ← back · O list of slides · F full screen · D light / dark · every figure can be dragged and switched

The argument in five steps

  1. Rewrite the classical formulas: each weight is a function with poles near its own vertex, divided by a function with poles on the whole boundary.
  2. The denominator is the canonical function of the domain, which positive geometries attach to a region by one rule.
  3. The numerators are canonical functions too, of an infinite wedge at each vertex. That the weights sum to one then needs no computation.
  4. The adjoint is the polynomial that appears over a common denominator.
  5. How far does it reach? Three open problems of the paper, and what has been published since 2019.

A map of the topics

Part 1

The pattern

Segment, triangle, convex polygon: three classical recipes, written so that their poles show.

Interpolation on a segment, written with poles

Linear interpolation, divided above and below by (x-a)(b-x):

f(x)=\frac{\dfrac{1}{x-a}\,f(a)+\dfrac{1}{b-x}\,f(b)}{\dfrac{1}{x-a}+\dfrac{1}{b-x}}
  • Each data value is weighted by a function with a pole at its own point.
  • The denominator has simple poles at both ends: \frac{1}{x-a}+\frac{1}{b-x}=\frac{b-a}{(x-a)(b-x)}.
  • Interpolation happens because the weights blow up, as in Shepard interpolation.

The same trick on a triangle

\lambda_i=A_i/A, with A_i the area of the small triangle opposite \mathbf p_i. Divide by A_0A_1A_2:

f(\mathbf x)=\frac{\dfrac{f(\mathbf p_0)}{A_1A_2}+\dfrac{f(\mathbf p_1)}{A_2A_0}+\dfrac{f(\mathbf p_2)}{A_0A_1}}{\dfrac{A}{A_0A_1A_2}}
  • Each A_i is linear in \mathbf x and vanishes on the side opposite \mathbf p_i.
  • The weight of \mathbf p_0 has poles on the two sides that meet at \mathbf p_0.
  • The denominator has poles on all three sides.

Convex polygons: Wachspress coordinates

Generalised barycentric coordinates: \lambda_i(\mathbf x)\ge0 with

\sum_i\lambda_i(\mathbf x)=1,\qquad \sum_i\lambda_i(\mathbf x)\,\mathbf p_i=\mathbf x .

Three equations for n unknowns: unique for a triangle, a choice for n>3. Wachspress’ rational choice:

\lambda_i=\frac{w_i}{\sum_k w_k},\qquad w_i(\mathbf x)=\frac{C_i}{A_{i-1}(\mathbf x)\,A_i(\mathbf x)}

A_i: area of the triangle \mathbf x\,\mathbf p_i\,\mathbf p_{i+1} on edge i; C_i: area of the corner triangle \mathbf p_{i-1}\mathbf p_i\,\mathbf p_{i+1}.

The common pattern. A weight is a function with poles on the part of the boundary through its vertex, divided by the sum of all such functions, which has poles on the whole boundary.

Two questions are left open:

  • Why these particular functions?
  • Why does their sum behave so well?

Positive geometries answer both.

Part 2

Positive geometries and canonical forms

A rule that assigns to a region a function with poles on its whole boundary.

Positive geometries (Arkani-Hamed, Bai, Lam 2017)

A region X_{\ge0} with a unique rational canonical form \Omega(X_{\ge0})=C\,dx\,dy. Its canonical function C is singled out by two rules:

  • Poles on the boundary, and only there. Near each boundary piece C grows like 1/distance (a simple pole).
  • Recursion. Multiply by the distance to a boundary piece. What is left on that piece, the residue, is the canonical function of the piece itself. The recursion ends at points, with \pm1.

So the canonical function of a polygon is fixed by those of its edges, and those of the edges by their end points.

Origin: particle scattering. For certain theories the amplitudes are canonical forms of positive geometries such as the amplituhedron.

The basic examples

Segment — the function of Part 1:

\Omega([a,b])=\frac{(b-a)\,dx}{(x-a)(b-x)}

Triangle — the denominator of Part 1, times A/2:

\Omega(\triangle)=\frac{A^{2}}{2\,A_0A_1A_2}\;dx\,dy

Curved sides are allowed: half disc, pizza slice.

Residues: the boundary of a boundary

  • Sample C along a line at distance \varepsilon from an edge: it grows like 1/\varepsilon.
  • \varepsilon\,C converges to the canonical function of the edge, \dfrac{L}{s(L-s)}.
  • One more residue, at the ends of the edge: +1 and -1.

For interpolation: the construction on a polygon restricts to linear interpolation along each edge.

Triangulations, and a small zoo

Additivity. Cut a region into pieces: the canonical forms of the pieces add up. The poles at the cut have opposite residues and cancel:

\underbrace{\frac{1}{x-a}+\frac{1}{c-x}}_{[a,c]}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{x-c}+\frac{1}{b-x}}_{[c,b]} =\frac{1}{x-a}+\frac{1}{b-x}.

Hence convex polygons and polytopes are positive geometries: triangulate and add.

Generalised simplices — constant numerator

  • simplices in projective space
  • regions bounded by a conic and a line
  • positive parts of toric varieties
  • positive Grassmannians

Generalised polytopes — non-constant numerator

  • convex polytopes
  • polycons: convex regions bounded by lines and conics
  • Grassmann polytopes, in particular amplituhedra

Part 3

Barycentric coordinates as ratios of canonical forms

The denominators were canonical functions of the whole domain. The numerators are canonical functions as well.

The recipe for the weight of a vertex

  1. Keep only the pieces of the boundary that meet at the vertex.
  2. Close them up with one extra side, infinitely far away as seen from the evaluation point: the point or line x^* dual to x. It adds no pole at any finite point.
  3. Divide the canonical function of this region by that of the whole shape.

Segment. The half line from a has canonical function 1/(x-a), and

\lambda_a(x)=\frac{\Omega([a,x^*])}{\Omega([a,b])} =\frac{1}{x-a}\cdot\frac{(x-a)(b-x)}{b-a}=\frac{b-x}{b-a}.

Polygons: wedge over polygon

\lambda_i(\mathbf x)=\frac{\Omega\big(\triangle(l_{i-1},\,l_i,\,\mathbf x^*)\big)}{\Omega(P)}
w_i=\frac{\det(\mathbf n_{i-1},\mathbf n_i)}{h_{i-1}(\mathbf x)\,h_i(\mathbf x)},\qquad C_P=\sum_i w_i
  • h_k: distance to the edge line l_k; \mathbf n_k: its normal.
  • The Wachspress weights: C_i/(A_{i-1}A_i)=2w_i.
  • At \mathbf p_i, w_i outgrows all others: \lambda_i\to1.

Why the weights sum to one

The claim is \sum_i w_i=C_P. But the wedges are not a subdivision: each contains the whole polygon, and they overlap.

First answer: cancelling poles. Projectively, every wedge is a triangle with a third side \mathbf x^* at infinity. In the sum these poles at infinity cancel, like the poles at a cut. The wedges form a signed triangulation of P.

The partition of unity is the statement that the pieces of a tiling add up to the whole.

Second answer: a picture. Replace every edge line by the point

\mathbf d_k=\frac{\mathbf n_k}{h_k(\mathbf x)}

The \mathbf d_k span the polar dual P^*_{\mathbf x}, and the wedges become triangles that tile it:

\operatorname{Area}(\mathbf 0,\mathbf d_{i-1},\mathbf d_i)=\frac{w_i}{2},\qquad C_P(\mathbf x)=2\operatorname{Area}(P^*_{\mathbf x})

This recovers Wachspress coordinates as ratios of polar dual volumes (Ju, Schaefer, Warren, Desbrun). In dimension d: C_P=d!\operatorname{Vol}(P^*_{\mathbf x}).

A mechanical reading: the wheel Added remark

  • Edges = rim bars, \mathbf x = hub, spokes to the vertices.
  • One self-stress: spoke tension w_i per unit length, rim compression 1/h_i.
  • The dual polygon is Maxwell’s force diagram.
  • Only Wachspress coordinates are balanced by the rim alone.

One dimension up Added illustration

In a simple polyhedron three face planes meet at a vertex and bound an infinite cone, which takes the place of the wedge:

w_v=\frac{|\det(\mathbf n_a,\mathbf n_b,\mathbf n_c)|}{h_a\,h_b\,h_c},\qquad \lambda_v=\frac{w_v}{C_P}
C_P=\sum_v w_v=3!\,\operatorname{Vol}(P^*_{\mathbf x})

The numerator: the adjoint polynomial

C_P(\mathbf x)=\frac{\operatorname{adj}_P(\mathbf x)}{h_0(\mathbf x)\,h_1(\mathbf x)\cdots h_{n-1}(\mathbf x)}
  • Degree n-3: the parts of highest degree cancel (the poles at infinity again).
  • Two non-adjacent edge lines cross outside the polygon; there the numerator must vanish.
  • There are n(n-3)/2 such points, exactly as many as fix a curve of degree n-3.
  • The adjoint curve never enters the polygon.

Wachspress; Warren’s definition through triangulations.

Three open problems of the paper

Positive geometries exist well beyond polytopes. How far does the recipe reach?

  1. Line space. Can barycentric coordinates for lines be defined as ratios of canonical forms on the Grassmannian G(2,4)?
  2. Mean value coordinates. They are not rational. Is there a “canonical function” of a disc, and a wider class of geometries with non-rational forms?
  3. Splines and integral geometry. The dual-volume integral is the Laplace transform of an indicator function, as in the theory of simplex, box and cone splines. One theory for splines and barycentric interpolants?

Open problem 1: interpolation in line space

  • Lines in 3D form a four-dimensional space, the Grassmannian G(2,4).
  • Plücker coordinates p_{ij}: six numbers up to scale, with p_{12}p_{34}-p_{13}p_{24}+p_{14}p_{23}=0.
  • The part with positive Plücker coordinates is a positive geometry.
  • The role of the triangle: the tetrahedral line complex. The six edge lines of a tetrahedron take the place of the vertices and form the pattern of an octahedron.

As of November 2026 we found no published construction of barycentric coordinates in line space.

Open problem 2: mean value coordinates and the disc

  • A disc is not a positive geometry: its boundary has no corners, so the recursion never arrives at points.
  • Still, there is a natural function singular along a conic:
C\;\propto\;\frac{1}{(1-x^2-y^2)^{3/2}}
  • It is a dual volume: the polar dual of a disc is an ellipse.
  • It is not rational, and grows like 1/h^{3/2} at distance h from the circle, not like 1/h.

Curved boundaries: polypols Later development

\Omega(P)=\frac{\operatorname{adj}_P(x,y)}{f_1(x,y)\cdots f_k(x,y)}\;dx\,dy
  • Quasi-regular rational polypols are positive geometries (Kohn et al.).
  • The adjoint passes through the residual points.
  • Wachspress’ conjecture, that the adjoint of a regular polycon stays outside it, is false: Brüser (2026), three ellipses.

Wachspress’ basis on a polycon Later development

Pizza slice: corner O, vertices V_1,V_2 on the circle, a side node M on the arc.

W_O=\frac{Q}{D},\quad W_M=k_M\frac{L_1L_2}{D},\quad W_{V_1}=k_1\frac{L_2\,\ell_1}{D}
  • Q=1-x^2-y^2, adjoint D=1+2y.
  • W_O is the wedge recipe of Part 3.
  • The others are not canonical forms of sub-regions in any obvious way, and depend on the arbitrary position of M.

Why a canonical form exists: mixed Hodge theory Later development

Brown, Dupont (2025). A region whose boundary lies on Y\subset X is a class in the relative homology group H_n(X,Y). Its mixed Hodge structure supplies two numbers:

\operatorname{cr}=h^{0,0},\qquad g=\sum_{p>0}h^{-p,0}
\dim\Omega^n_{\log}(X\setminus Y)=\operatorname{cr}+g
  • The combinatorial rank counts the canonical forms that exist.
  • The genus counts the ways in which they fail to be unique.
  • Genus zero: the canonical form is the only logarithmic form with residue \pm1 at the corners of the region and 0 at the others.

Theorems, not assumptions: additivity, residues (for boundary pieces of genus zero), push-forwards, products.

Polygons. With edge functions f_i:

\varpi_P=-\sum_i d\log f_i\wedge d\log f_{i+1}

One term per corner: this is the wedge decomposition of Part 3, and the Wachspress coordinate of a vertex is the share of its corner.

Hodge theory is silent on positivity inside the domain; for that the adjoint remains the tool. Genus zero is sufficient but not necessary (Koefler, Pavlov, Sinn).

A corner makes the form, a handle destroys its uniqueness

  • \varepsilon=0: a nodal cubic. The teardrop has one corner; the residue of its form along the boundary has a simple pole at each end, the form of a segment.
  • \varepsilon>0: a smooth cubic of genus one. The residue is finite all the way round: it is the holomorphic form of the curve.
  • That form has become the ambiguity, and the oval has no canonical form.

Rank and genus of plane curves

g=\sum_{\text{components}}\text{genus},\qquad \operatorname{cr}=\sum_{P}\big(r_P-1\big)-(k-1)

Dual volumes become measures Later development

Take the polar dual P^* about the origin, once and for all:

C(\mathbf x)=2\int_{P^*}\frac{\mu(\mathbf y)\,d\mathbf y}{(1+\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf y)^{3}}
  • Polygon: \mu\equiv1, the dual-volume formula.
  • Disc cut by a line: the same formula with a density 0\le\mu\le1 on the dual region (Mazzucchelli, Raman 2025).
  • For the unit disc the transform is 2\pi(1-u^2-v^2)^{-3/2}: algebraic, not rational (Shapiro 2026).

The cone behind the dual polygon Added remark

Write h_i(\mathbf x)=\langle \mathbf a_i,(1,\mathbf x)\rangle. The vectors \mathbf a_i span a cone C\subset\mathbb R^3, and

C_P(\mathbf x)=\mathcal L\big[\mathbf 1_C\big](1,\mathbf x)
  • The canonical function is the Laplace transform of the indicator function of the cone: the third open problem.
  • The cone does not depend on \mathbf x. The point only chooses the cutting plane; the cut is the dual polygon.

Splines: the same transform, read backwards Added remark

T: the multivariate spline (truncated power) of the vectors \mathbf a_i, a second function on the same cone.

\mathcal L\big[\mathbf 1_C\big](1,\mathbf x)=\frac{\operatorname{adj}_P(\mathbf x)}{\prod_i h_i(\mathbf x)}
\mathcal L\big[T\big](1,\mathbf x)=\frac{1}{\prod_i h_i(\mathbf x)}
  • Uniform density on the dual polygon: the canonical function.
  • Spline density: its denominator alone.
  • The adjoint is the ratio of the two measurements.

The adjoint as a differential operator Added remark

Under the Laplace transform, multiplying by \langle\mathbf u,\mathbf a\rangle is differentiating in the direction \mathbf a, and

D_{\mathbf a_k}T_{\{\mathbf a_i\}}=T_{\{\mathbf a_i\}\setminus\mathbf a_k}
\operatorname{adj}(\partial)\,T=\sum_\sigma \mathbf 1_{\operatorname{cone}(\sigma)}=\mathbf 1_C
  • Each derivative removes one vector.
  • The spline is a tent-like function of degree n-3 on the dual polygon; the adjoint is the operator of order n-3 that flattens it to the constant one.

One variable: B-splines and poles Added remark

The B-spline M with knots t_0,\dots,t_n is the density whose transform has a pole at every knot:

\int M(y)\,\frac{dy}{(x-y)^{n+1}}=\frac{1}{\prod_{i=0}^{n}(x-t_i)}
\frac{1}{\prod_i (x-t_i)}=\sum_i\frac{c_i}{x-t_i},\qquad c_i=\prod_{j\ne i}\frac{1}{t_i-t_j}
  • The partial-fraction coefficients c_i are the weights of Lagrange’s barycentric formula.

Toric and symplectic geometry Added remark

  • Both functions on the cone are Duistermaat–Heckman measures.
  • The spline belongs to a torus acting on \mathbb C^n: its moment map is \mathbf z\mapsto\sum_k\tfrac12|z_k|^2\,\mathbf a_k, and the fixed-point formula gives 1/\prod_k\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf a_k\rangle.
  • The value of the spline is the volume of the fibre polytope \{\mathbf t\ge0:\ \sum_k t_k\mathbf a_k=\mathbf v\}.
  • Crossing a diagonal of the dual polygon changes the shape of the fibre: this is why the spline changes its formula there.

Volume minimisation Added remark

  • In toric geometry the canonical function is a volume that geometers and physicists minimise:
\operatorname{vol}(\mathbf b)=\frac{\pi^3}{27}\,C_P(\mathbf x)
  • The minimum is the Santaló point: there the centroid of the dual polygon is \mathbf x.

More nodes on a line: the barycentric formula Added remark

Nodes t_0<\dots<t_n, node polynomial \ell(x)=\prod_j(x-t_j):

\lambda_j(x)=\frac{c_j}{x-t_j}\Big/\sum_k\frac{c_k}{x-t_k},\qquad c_j=\frac{1}{\ell'(t_j)}
  • \sum_k c_k/(x-t_k)=1/\ell(x): the weights are the residues of 1/\ell.
  • Lift the nodes to the moment curve: the \lambda_j are the simplex coordinates of (x,x^2,\dots,x^n).
  • The curve touches the simplex at the vertices only, so beyond two nodes some coordinate is negative.

The residue theorem at work Added remark

\omega=\frac{\ell(x)}{\ell(t)}\,\frac{g(t)\,dt}{x-t}
  • Poles: the nodes and the point x.
  • If g has degree at most n, there is no pole at infinity and the residues sum to zero:
\sum_j\lambda_j(x)\,g(t_j)=g(x)

One formula, four choices of weights Added remark

Weights w_j in place of c_j. Berrut’s weights (-1)^j give a canonical form after all:

\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^j}{x-t_j}=C_A(x)

A=[t_0,t_1]\cup[t_2,t_3]\cup\dots\cup[t_{n-1},t_n], every second interval.

Hermite’s contour integral for the error Added remark

f(x)-p(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_\Gamma\frac{\ell(x)}{\ell(t)}\,\frac{f(t)}{t-x}\,dt

For a rational f with poles \pi_k and residues \rho_k:

f(x)-p(x)=\sum_k\rho_k\,\frac{\ell(x)}{\ell(\pi_k)\,(x-\pi_k)}

Small inside the level curve of |\ell| through the pole; outside it, Runge’s phenomenon.

Two triangulations from one contour Added remark

C_P(\mathbf x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{\prod_{j=0}^{3}\,(c_j-i\,s\,k_j)}
  • Closing the contour above or below gives the two triangulations; that they agree is Cauchy’s theorem (Arkani-Hamed, Bai, Lam).

Mean value coordinates: area versus perimeter Later development

One dual polygon, two measurements (after Schaefer, Ju, Warren):

\sum_i w_i^{\mathrm{W}}=\sum_i\frac{L_i}{r_i}=2\operatorname{Area}(P^*_{\mathbf x})
\sum_i w_i^{\mathrm{MV}}=\sum_i L_i=\operatorname{Perimeter}(P^*_{\mathbf x})
  • L_i: length of the dual edge of \mathbf p_i; r_i=|\mathbf p_i-\mathbf x|.
  • On a triangle the two sets of coordinates coincide.

The smooth limit: the disc Later development

For the unit disc and a point at distance r from the centre, the dual is an ellipse:

2\operatorname{Area}=\frac{2\pi}{(1-r^2)^{3/2}},\qquad \operatorname{Perimeter}=\frac{4\,E(r)}{1-r^2}
  • The disc function of open problem 2 is the continuous limit of the Wachspress normaliser.
  • The mean value analogue is an elliptic integral.
  • Wachspress coordinates of inscribed regular polygons converge to the smooth kernel 1/(1-\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf x)^2 (Warren, Schaefer, Hirani, Desbrun).

Push-forwards and the bilinear patch Later development

C_{\text{quad}}(\mathbf x)=\!\!\sum_{B(u,v)=\mathbf x}\!\!\frac{1}{u(1-u)\,v(1-v)\,\det\!\big(\partial_uB,\partial_vB\big)}
  • The bilinear patch B carries the unit square onto the quadrilateral; both preimages of \mathbf x must be kept.
  • Each term contains a square root; only the sum is rational.

Line space: what is known Later development

\Omega\big(G_{\ge0}(2,4)\big)=\frac{da\,db\,dc\,dd}{b\,c\,(ad-bc)},\qquad p_{12}=1,\;p_{23}=c,\;p_{34}=ad-bc,\;p_{14}=b .
  • Four facets, not six. The poles are where a cyclically consecutive Plücker coordinate vanishes; p_{13} and p_{24} cannot vanish inside. The symmetry is cyclic, not that of a tetrahedron.
  • The octahedron is a moment-map image. The six numbers p_{ij}/\sum p_{kl} look like barycentric coordinates, but they map a four-dimensional region onto a three-dimensional one: a point of the octahedron does not determine a line.
  • A first adjoint in line space. Pavlov and Ranestad (2025): one more hyperplane cuts out a “positive pentahedron” with a unique adjoint, \sum\max(c_{ij},0)\,p_{ij}. Loosely, the step from a triangle to a quadrilateral.

The chart formula is our own evaluation of eq. (5.40) of Arkani-Hamed, Bai and Lam.

Summary

  • Linear, triangular and Wachspress interpolation share one pattern: a weight with poles at its vertex, over a function with poles on the whole boundary.
  • The denominator is the canonical function of the domain; the numerators are canonical functions of wedges.
  • Partition of unity is additivity of canonical forms, or a tiling of the polar dual.
  • The adjoint is what remains over a common denominator; it must stay outside the domain.
  • Open: curved domains, non-rational coordinates, line space.
