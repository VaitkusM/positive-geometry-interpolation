Positive Geometries for Barycentric Interpolation

after Márton Vaitkus, Budapest University of Technology and Economics

To interpolate is to fill in values between given data. The three classical recipes — linear interpolation on a segment, barycentric coordinates on a triangle and Wachspress coordinates on a convex polygon — share a structure that is easy to miss: every weight is a ratio of two functions that become infinite on the boundary of the domain. Positive geometries, a notion from theoretical physics, say where these functions come from and why the recipe works. All figures on this page are interactive.

Two ways into this page

Coming from geometric modelling or graphics? You know barycentric coordinates; Sections 1–3 only rewrite them in an unusual way. The new material is in Section 4, where the grey “background” boxes introduce the few notions needed about differential forms and residues.

Coming from pure mathematics or physics? You may know forms, residues and perhaps the amplituhedron. Sections 1–3 explain what interpolation people want from “barycentric coordinates”; Section 5 shows that canonical forms deliver exactly that.

The argument in five steps

  1. Sections 1–3 rewrite the classical formulas so that each weight reads “a function that is infinite near its own vertex, divided by a function that is infinite on the whole boundary”.
  2. Section 4 identifies the denominator: it is the canonical function of the domain, which positive geometries attach to a region by one simple rule.
  3. Section 5 identifies the numerators: they are canonical functions too, of an infinite wedge at each vertex. That the weights sum to one then needs no computation.
  4. Section 6 looks at the polynomial that appears when everything is put over a common denominator, the adjoint.
  5. Sections 7 and 8 ask how far the idea reaches: the paper’s open problems, and what has been published since.

Three words used throughout

A rational function is a quotient of two polynomials. It has a pole where its denominator vanishes and the function becomes infinite. The pole is simple when the function grows like 1/distance on approaching it, as 1/x does at x=0, and not faster.

1 Interpolation on a segment, written with poles

Given values f(a) and f(b) at the two ends of a segment, linear interpolation fills in the values in between:

f(x)=\underbrace{\frac{b-x}{b-a}}_{\lambda_a(x)}\,f(a)+\underbrace{\frac{x-a}{b-a}}_{\lambda_b(x)}\,f(b).

The weights \lambda_a,\lambda_b are the barycentric coordinates of x. They are positive inside the segment and sum to one, and at the end point a they take the values \lambda_a=1, \lambda_b=0, so that the data value f(a) is reproduced there.

Now a small algebraic step that the rest of the page builds on. Write the formula as one fraction, \big((b-x)f(a)+(x-a)f(b)\big)/(b-a), and divide top and bottom by (x-a)(b-x). The function does not change, but it now reads

f(x)=\frac{\dfrac{1}{x-a}\,f(a)+\dfrac{1}{b-x}\,f(b)}{\dfrac{1}{x-a}+\dfrac{1}{b-x}} \qquad\text{with}\qquad \frac{1}{x-a}+\frac{1}{b-x}=\frac{b-a}{(x-a)(b-x)}.

This is called the barycentric form. Each data value is weighted by a function with a pole at its own point: 1/(x-a) is infinite at a, and 1/(b-x) at b. As x approaches a, the term 1/(x-a) outgrows everything else in both numerator and denominator, so the ratio tends to f(a). Interpolation happens because the weights blow up. This is the idea of Shepard interpolation, f(x)=\sum_i w_i(x)f(x_i)\,/\sum_j w_j(x), with weights such as w_i=1/\operatorname{dist}(x,x_i).

Added remark With more than two data points the same form of the formula gives polynomial and rational interpolation on a line. That case, and its relation to residues and contour integrals, is taken up in Section 8.2.

2 The same trick on a triangle

On a triangle \mathbf p_0\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2 the barycentric coordinates of a point \mathbf x are ratios of areas. The point \mathbf x splits the triangle into three small triangles. Write A_i for the area of the one spanned by \mathbf x and the side opposite to \mathbf p_i, and A=A_0+A_1+A_2 for the total area. Then \lambda_i=A_i/A, and the same division trick as on the segment, this time by A_0A_1A_2, gives

f(\mathbf x)=\frac{A_0\,f(\mathbf p_0)+A_1\,f(\mathbf p_1)+A_2\,f(\mathbf p_2)}{A} \;=\;\frac{\dfrac{1}{A_1A_2}f(\mathbf p_0)+\dfrac{1}{A_2A_0}f(\mathbf p_1)+\dfrac{1}{A_0A_1}f(\mathbf p_2)}{\dfrac{A}{A_0A_1A_2}}.

Where are the poles? Each area A_i is half a side length times the distance from \mathbf x to that side, so it is a linear function of \mathbf x that vanishes exactly on the side opposite to \mathbf p_i. The weight 1/(A_1A_2) of \mathbf p_0 is therefore infinite on the sides opposite to \mathbf p_1 and \mathbf p_2, and these are the two sides that meet at \mathbf p_0. The denominator is infinite on all three sides. This is the picture of Section 1 again: a weight with poles at the vertex’s own part of the boundary, divided by a function with poles on the whole boundary.

3 Convex polygons: Wachspress coordinates

For a convex polygon with vertices \mathbf p_0,\dots,\mathbf p_{n-1} one wants generalised barycentric coordinates: functions \lambda_i(\mathbf x)\ge 0 with

\sum_i\lambda_i(\mathbf x)=1\quad\text{(partition of unity)},\qquad \sum_i\lambda_i(\mathbf x)\,\mathbf p_i=\mathbf x\quad\text{(linear precision)}.

The first condition says that the weights are those of an average, so constant data are reproduced. The second says that the average of the vertices, taken with these weights, is the point \mathbf x itself; it follows that every linear function is reproduced exactly. Together these are three linear equations for n unknowns. For a triangle they have one solution, the area coordinates of Section 2. For n>3 there are many, and one has to choose.

Wachspress found the simplest rational choice. The notation changes slightly from Section 2: A_i is now the area of the triangle \mathbf x\,\mathbf p_i\,\mathbf p_{i+1} on edge i, and C_i is the area of the corner triangle \mathbf p_{i-1}\mathbf p_i\,\mathbf p_{i+1} (indices modulo n). Then

\lambda_i(\mathbf x)=\frac{w_i(\mathbf x)}{\sum_k w_k(\mathbf x)},\qquad w_i(\mathbf x)=\frac{C_i}{A_{i-1}(\mathbf x)\,A_i(\mathbf x)}.

The area A_{i-1} vanishes on the edge arriving at \mathbf p_i and A_i on the edge leaving it. So once more the weight of a vertex has poles along the two edges through that vertex, and the normalising sum has poles along the whole boundary. For n=3 all C_i equal the area of the triangle and the formula is the one of Section 2. You can experiment with these coordinates in Figure 5.

The common pattern. In all three cases a weight is a function with poles on the part of the boundary through its vertex, divided by the sum of all such functions, which has poles on the whole boundary. Interpolation happens because numerator and denominator blow up together. Two questions are left open: why these particular functions, and why does their sum behave so well? Positive geometries answer both.

4 Positive geometries and canonical forms

Sections 1–3 produced, for each domain, a function with poles on its whole boundary: 1/(x-a)+1/(b-x) for the segment, A/(A_0A_1A_2) for the triangle, \sum_k w_k for the polygon. Is there a rule that assigns such a function to a region? There is, and it comes from an unexpected place.

Positive geometries were introduced by Arkani-Hamed, Bai and Lam in the study of particle scattering. Informally, a positive geometry is a region X_{\ge0} (a segment, a polygon, a region bounded by lines and conics, …) that comes with a unique canonical form \Omega(X_{\ge0}). On this page you can think of the form as a function C on the region, its canonical function (the first background box says why). It is a rational function singled out by two rules:

  • Poles on the boundary, and only there. Near each piece of the boundary the function grows like 1/distance (a simple pole); it is finite everywhere else.
  • Recursion. Remove the pole at a boundary piece by multiplying with the distance to it. What is left on that piece, the residue, must be the canonical function of the piece itself, which is a positive geometry of one dimension less. The recursion ends at points, whose canonical form is \pm1.

So the canonical function of a polygon is fixed by those of its edges, and those of the edges by their end points. The examples below make this concrete.

Background: differential forms and residues, in two paragraphs

On this page a differential form on a planar region is simply an expression \Omega=C(x,y)\,dx\,dy — a density that could be integrated — and all the information is in the function C, called the canonical function. On a segment the form is C(x)\,dx. We always draw and compute C.

If C blows up like 1/h near a boundary line, where h is the distance to that line, the residue along the line is what remains after removing the pole: \lim_{h\to0}h\,C, a function on the line. It is the several-variable version of the residue of complex analysis.

Background: the precise definition, and a remark on normalisation

A positive geometry is a pair (X,X_{\ge0}), where X is a d-dimensional complex projective variety and X_{\ge0} an oriented semi-algebraic subset of its real part, together with a unique rational d-form \Omega(X,X_{\ge0}) such that: for d=0, X is a point and \Omega=\pm1 according to orientation; for d>0, every boundary component is a positive geometry whose canonical form is the residue of \Omega along it. The same ambient space X carries many positive geometries.

In projective space with homogeneous coordinates the paper writes \Omega=C(\mathbf x)\,\omega(\mathbf x) with a projectively invariant measure \omega. This page works in an affine chart and writes \Omega=C\,dx\,dy. The two normalisations of C differ by a constant factor, which cancels in every ratio below.

The basic examples

Segment. The function met in Section 1 is the canonical function of [a,b]:

\Omega([a,b])=\frac{(b-a)\,dx}{(x-a)(b-x)}=\left(\frac{1}{x-a}+\frac{1}{b-x}\right)dx,

Check the two rules. The only poles are at a and b, and both are simple. Near a the function is 1/(x-a) plus something finite; multiplying by x-a and letting x\to a leaves the residue +1. At b the same computation with x-b gives -1. The signs record that the two end points are met with opposite orientations; they play no role in what follows.

Triangle. With \langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w\rangle the determinant of the homogeneous coordinates [x:y:1] of three points (twice the signed area of their triangle),

\Omega(\triangle)=\frac{\langle\mathbf p_0,\mathbf p_1,\mathbf p_2\rangle^{2}} {\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf p_0,\mathbf p_1\rangle\,\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf p_1,\mathbf p_2\rangle\,\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf p_2,\mathbf p_0\rangle}\;dx\,dy \;=\;\frac{A^{2}}{2\,A_0A_1A_2}\;dx\,dy ,

which is the denominator of Section 2 multiplied by the constant A/2. The three factors below the line vanish on the three sides, so there is a simple pole along each side and nothing else. For the triangle with vertices (0,0),(1,0),(0,1) the formula becomes dx\,dy/\big(xy(1-x-y)\big): one factor for each of the sides x=0, y=0 and x+y=1.

Residues: the boundary of a boundary

The recursion rule can be watched at work. Take a polygon, and sample its canonical function C along a line parallel to one edge, at distance \varepsilon from it. As the line approaches the edge, C grows like 1/\varepsilon. Multiply by \varepsilon to remove this growth. In the limit \varepsilon\to0 one finds the canonical function of the edge: the segment formula of Section 1, which reads L/\big(s(L-s)\big) for an edge of length L and a point at distance s from its start. Taking residues once more, at the two ends of the edge, leaves +1 and -1, and the recursion has reached its end.

Triangulations and a small zoo

Canonical forms are additive: if a region is cut into pieces, its canonical form is the sum of the forms of the pieces. The smallest example is a segment [a,b] cut at a point c:

\underbrace{\frac{1}{x-a}+\frac{1}{c-x}}_{[a,c]}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{x-c}+\frac{1}{b-x}}_{[c,b]} =\frac{1}{x-a}+\frac{1}{b-x}.

The two middle terms are the poles at the cut. They have opposite residues and cancel, so the sum has no pole at c. The same happens along any interior cut of a planar region. Hence the canonical form of a convex polygon can be found by cutting it into triangles and adding their forms, and convex polytopes are positive geometries. Section 5 uses additivity in a less obvious way, with pieces that overlap.

Positive geometries are far more general than polygons. The two lists below show the range; the unfamiliar names in them are not needed later.

Generalised simplices — constant numerator:

  • simplices in projective space;
  • regions bounded by a conic and a line (the half disc in Figure 3);
  • positive parts of toric varieties;
  • positive Grassmannians (totally positive matrices).

Generalised polytopes — non-constant numerator:

  • convex polytopes;
  • polycons: convex regions bounded by lines and conics (the pizza slice);
  • Grassmann polytopes, in particular amplituhedra.
Background: why physicists care

In the study of particle scattering, probabilities are computed from rational functions of the particle momenta whose poles correspond to physical particles. For certain theories these functions turn out to be canonical forms of positive geometries such as the amplituhedron: roughly, “probability ≈ volume”. Different ways of organising the calculation correspond to different triangulations of the same shape, in the same way that Section 5 obtains one set of coordinates from different decompositions.

5 Barycentric coordinates as ratios of canonical forms

Section 4 identified the denominators of Sections 1–3 as canonical functions of the whole domain. The proposal of the paper is that the numerators are canonical functions as well, of a simpler region attached to each vertex. The recipe for the weight of a vertex has three steps.

  1. Keep only the pieces of the boundary that meet at the vertex.
  2. Close them up into a region with one extra side, placed infinitely far away as seen from the evaluation point. Because it is infinitely far away, it adds no pole at any finite point.
  3. Divide the canonical function of this region by the canonical function of the whole shape.

In projective geometry the extra side of step 2 has a name: it is the point or line x^* dual to the evaluation point x. If the drawing is centred at x, this dual is the point or line at infinity, and that is all one needs to know about it here.

Segment. The only piece of the boundary at a is the point a itself. Closing up with a point at infinity gives the half line from a to infinity. Its canonical function is 1/(x-a): let b\to\infty in the segment formula 1/(x-a)+1/(b-x) and the second term disappears. Step 3 then gives

\lambda_a(x)=\frac{\Omega([a,x^*])}{\Omega([a,b])} =\frac{1}{x-a}\cdot\frac{(x-a)(b-x)}{b-a}=\frac{b-x}{b-a},

the linear interpolation weight of Section 1.

Triangle and polygon. Let l_{i-1} and l_i be the lines of the two edges through \mathbf p_i. Closing them up with the line at infinity \mathbf x^* gives a “triangle” with one side at infinity: the infinite wedge between the two edge lines (shaded in Figure 5). The recipe says

\lambda_i(\mathbf x)=\frac{\Omega\big(\triangle(l_{i-1},\,l_i,\,\mathbf x^*)\big)}{\Omega(P)} .

To make this explicit, let h_k(\mathbf x) be the distance from \mathbf x to the line l_k and \mathbf n_k the unit normal of that line pointing into the polygon. The canonical function of the wedge has a simple pole on each of its two lines and a constant numerator, and the canonical function of the polygon is the sum over all vertices:

w_i(\mathbf x)=\frac{\det(\mathbf n_{i-1},\mathbf n_i)}{h_{i-1}(\mathbf x)\,h_i(\mathbf x)}, \qquad\text{and}\qquad \Omega(P)=\sum_i w_i(\mathbf x)\;dx\,dy .

The constant \det(\mathbf n_{i-1},\mathbf n_i) is the sine of the angle by which the boundary turns at \mathbf p_i; it is what makes the residues at the corner equal to \pm1.

These w_i are the Wachspress weights of Section 3 in different notation. The area A_k there is half the length of edge k times h_k, and the corner area C_i is half the product of the two edge lengths at \mathbf p_i times the same sine. In C_i/(A_{i-1}A_i) the edge lengths cancel and 2w_i remains. The factor 2 is common to all weights and drops out, so the ratios \lambda_i=w_i/\sum_k w_k are the barycentric coordinates of a triangle and the Wachspress coordinates of a convex polygon.

The interpolation property can now be read off from the poles. At the vertex \mathbf p_i both h_{i-1} and h_i vanish, so w_i outgrows all other weights and \lambda_i\to1. On the edge from \mathbf p_i to \mathbf p_{i+1} only w_i and w_{i+1} have a pole, so only these two coordinates survive, and by the residue property of Figure 4 they are the linear interpolation weights of Section 1.

Why the weights sum to one

The displayed formula contains a claim that deserves a second look: the wedge functions add up to the canonical function of the polygon, \sum_i w_i=C_P. Once this is known, \sum_i\lambda_i=\sum_i w_i/C_P=1 is immediate. But the wedges are not a subdivision of the polygon. Each of them contains the whole polygon, and they overlap each other. Why should their forms add up?

First answer: cancelling poles. Seen projectively, every wedge is a triangle with a third side \mathbf x^* at infinity, and its form has a pole there. In the sum over all vertices these poles at infinity cancel, in the same way as the poles at the cut point c cancelled in Section 4. What remains has poles only on the edges of the polygon, with the right residues. In the language of positive geometries the wedges form a signed triangulation of P, and additivity holds for those as well.

Second answer: a picture. There is a second drawing in which the overlapping wedges turn into triangles that fit together without overlap. Move the origin to \mathbf x and replace every edge line l_k by the point

\mathbf d_k=\frac{\mathbf n_k}{h_k(\mathbf x)} ,

which lies in the direction of the normal, at distance 1/h_k: the closer the edge, the farther the point. The polygon with vertices \mathbf d_k is the polar dual P^*_{\mathbf x} of P with respect to \mathbf x (right panel of Figure 5). Lines of P have become points, and the vertex \mathbf p_i, where two lines meet, has become the edge joining \mathbf d_{i-1} and \mathbf d_i. The triangle from the origin to that edge has area

\operatorname{Area}(\mathbf 0,\mathbf d_{i-1},\mathbf d_i)=\tfrac12\det(\mathbf d_{i-1},\mathbf d_i) =\frac{\det(\mathbf n_{i-1},\mathbf n_i)}{2\,h_{i-1}h_i}=\frac{w_i}{2}.

So each wedge weight is twice the area of a triangle, these triangles tile the dual polygon in the ordinary sense, and

C_P(\mathbf x)=\sum_i w_i(\mathbf x)=2\operatorname{Area}(P^*_{\mathbf x}), \qquad \lambda_i(\mathbf x)=\frac{\operatorname{Area}(\mathbf 0,\mathbf d_{i-1},\mathbf d_i)}{\operatorname{Area}(P^*_{\mathbf x})}.

The partition of unity is now the statement that the pieces of a tiling add up to the whole. This recovers the description of Wachspress coordinates as ratios of polar dual volumes by Ju, Schaefer, Warren and Desbrun.

The statement holds in every dimension: the canonical function of a convex polytope at \mathbf x is, in the normalisation of this page, d! times the volume of its polar dual. The paper writes that volume as an integral in homogeneous coordinates, which will return in Sections 7 and 8.2:

\operatorname{Vol}(P^*_{\mathbf x})=\frac{1}{d!}\int_{\mathbf y\in P^*_{\mathbf x}}\frac{\omega(\mathbf y)}{(\mathbf x^{T}\mathbf y)^{d+1}} .

A mechanical reading: the wheel Added remark

In short: build the polygon as a wheel, with its edges as the rim and the point \mathbf x as the hub, joined to the vertices by spokes. Tighten the spokes. The tension in each spoke, per unit of its length, is its Wachspress weight, and the dual polygon is the diagram of the forces.

The word barycentric is mechanical in origin: Möbius read the coordinates \lambda_i as masses at the vertices whose centre of gravity is \mathbf x. An equivalent picture uses forces. Join \mathbf x to every vertex by a spoke that pulls with the force w_i\,(\mathbf p_i-\mathbf x), proportional to its length; the factor w_i is the stress of the spoke, its tension per unit length. Linear precision, written as \sum_i w_i(\mathbf p_i-\mathbf x)=\mathbf 0, says that these forces balance at \mathbf x. This holds for every set of barycentric coordinates and does not distinguish Wachspress’.

The other ends of the spokes do. Each spoke also pulls its vertex inwards, and something has to hold the vertex in place. Let the edges of the polygon do it: make them rigid bars, hinged at the vertices, so that the polygon becomes the rim of a wheel. A framework of bars that carries internal forces without any outside load is said to be in a state of self-stress. The wheel has exactly one, up to a common factor:

\text{spoke } i:\ \text{tension } w_i=\frac{\det(\mathbf n_{i-1},\mathbf n_i)}{h_{i-1}h_i}\ \text{per unit length}, \qquad \text{rim bar } i:\ \text{compression of magnitude } \frac{1}{h_i}.

So the Wachspress coordinates are the only barycentric coordinates whose spoke forces are balanced by the rim alone; with any other choice the vertices need outside support. Inside a convex polygon all spokes are in tension and all rim bars in compression, as in a bicycle wheel.

The dual polygon is the force diagram. Maxwell showed how to draw the forces of a framework in equilibrium as a second figure: turn every bar force by a right angle; the forces at each joint then form a closed polygon, and these polygons fit together into the reciprocal figure. For the wheel, the reciprocal figure is the right panel of Figure 5, shown again in Figure 5b below together with the forces:

  • the dual edge from \mathbf d_{i-1} to \mathbf d_i is the force in spoke i, of length w_i\,|\mathbf p_i-\mathbf x|;
  • the segment from the origin to \mathbf d_i is the force in rim bar i, of length 1/h_i;
  • the triangle \mathbf 0\,\mathbf d_{i-1}\mathbf d_i is the closed force polygon at the vertex \mathbf p_i, and the dual polygon is the closed force polygon at the hub.

“The dual polygon closes up” and “the forces at the hub balance” are the same statement, and the tiling of the dual polygon by triangles is the equilibrium of the whole wheel. There is a third way to see the points \mathbf d_i. Lift the hub to height one above the plane and leave the rim where it is: the wheel becomes a pyramid over the polygon. The face of the pyramid over edge i is the graph of the function h_i(\cdot)/h_i(\mathbf x), and its slope (gradient) is \mathbf n_i/h_i(\mathbf x)=\mathbf d_i. This correspondence between self-stresses, reciprocal figures and polyhedral surfaces above the framework goes back to Maxwell and Cremona (see Whiteley).

Why this produces rational functions. The equilibrium conditions of a bar framework are linear equations for the bar forces, with coefficients that are linear in the positions of the joints. Solving them needs only the four arithmetic operations, so the stresses of a framework are always rational functions of the positions. Classically, rational operations are also exactly those that can be carried out with a ruler, by joining points and intersecting lines (parallels allowed), whereas square roots need a compass. Mean value coordinates (Section 7) contain the distances |\mathbf p_i-\mathbf x|, hence square roots; they balance the hub but are not the self-stress of the wheel. Section 8.3 compares the force polygons.

That the spoke forces of any barycentric coordinates balance is standard, and so is Maxwell’s theory. The identification of the Wachspress weights with the self-stress of the wheel, and of the polar dual with its reciprocal figure, follows directly from the formulas above and is checked in the project’s tests; we did not find it stated in the literature we searched.

One dimension up Added illustration

The paper notes that the generalisation to higher dimensions is straightforward. In d dimensions the three-step recipe applies word for word to polytopes in which every vertex lies on exactly d facets. Such polytopes are called simple: a cube is simple, an octahedron is not, because four faces meet at each of its vertices. In space, three face planes meet at a vertex and bound an infinite cone, which takes the place of the wedge. For a polyhedron with outward unit face normals \mathbf n_f and distances h_f(\mathbf x) from \mathbf x to the face planes, the weight of a vertex on the faces a,b,c is the exact analogue of the planar formula, with three distances in place of two:

w_v(\mathbf x)=\frac{|\det(\mathbf n_a,\mathbf n_b,\mathbf n_c)|}{h_a(\mathbf x)\,h_b(\mathbf x)\,h_c(\mathbf x)}, \qquad C_P(\mathbf x)=\sum_v w_v(\mathbf x)=3!\,\operatorname{Vol}(P^*_{\mathbf x}), \qquad \lambda_v=\frac{w_v}{C_P}.

The force picture of the wheel carries over in part. Each vertex of the polyhedron corresponds to a triangular face of the dual, perpendicular to the line from \mathbf x to that vertex, and w_v=2\operatorname{Area}(\text{dual face})/|\mathbf v-\mathbf x|. Forces along these lines with the dual areas as magnitudes balance at \mathbf x, because the area vectors of a closed polyhedron sum to zero. There is no rim of bars in this case: a polyhedron such as the cube is not rigid as a bar framework.

6 The numerator: the adjoint polynomial

The canonical function of an n-gon is a sum of n fractions w_i, each with two of the distances h_k in its denominator. Putting them over the common denominator h_0h_1\cdots h_{n-1} gives

C_P(\mathbf x)=\frac{\operatorname{adj}_P(\mathbf x)}{h_0(\mathbf x)\,h_1(\mathbf x)\cdots h_{n-1}(\mathbf x)}, \qquad \operatorname{adj}_P(\mathbf x)=\sum_i\det(\mathbf n_{i-1},\mathbf n_i)\!\!\prod_{k\ne i-1,\,i}\!\!h_k(\mathbf x).

The numerator is the adjoint polynomial, introduced by Wachspress and later defined through triangulations by Warren. Each term of the sum is a product of n-2 linear functions, but the parts of highest degree cancel (this is the cancellation of the poles at infinity from Section 5), so the adjoint has degree n-3: a constant for a triangle, a line for a quadrilateral, a conic for a pentagon.

What is the adjoint for? The denominator vanishes on the full edge lines, not only on the edges, and two edge lines that are not neighbours cross somewhere outside the polygon. At such a crossing two factors of the denominator vanish at once, and the function would have a singularity that the rules of Section 4 do not allow: the residue along an edge line is the canonical function of that edge, and may be infinite only at the two end points of the edge. The numerator must therefore vanish at every such crossing. In other words, the adjoint curve \operatorname{adj}_P=0 passes through every intersection point of two non-adjacent edge lines.

This determines the curve. There are n(n-3)/2 such points (2 for a quadrilateral, 5 for a pentagon, 9 for a hexagon, counting points at infinity when two lines are parallel), which is exactly the number of points that in general fix a curve of degree n-3: two points fix a line, five a conic, nine a cubic.

7 Open problems

The framework unifies the adjoint, dual-volume and Shepard-like descriptions of Wachspress coordinates. Its real promise is that positive geometries exist well beyond polytopes. The paper ends with three directions.

Interpolation in line space

So far the things being interpolated between were points. Could they be lines? A line in space is fixed by four numbers (for instance, the two points where it crosses two fixed planes), so the set of all lines in 3D is a four-dimensional space. It is called the Grassmannian G(2,4). The standard way to give a line coordinates is to pick two points on it, write their homogeneous coordinates as the rows of a 2\times4 matrix, and take the six 2\times2 minors p_{ij}. These Plücker coordinates are defined up to a common factor and satisfy one quadratic equation, p_{12}p_{34}-p_{13}p_{24}+p_{14}p_{23}=0, so line space is a curved hypersurface (a quadric) in the five-dimensional projective space \mathbb P^5.

The part of line space where the Plücker coordinates are positive is a positive geometry, so it has a canonical form with poles along its boundary. What should play the role of a triangle? The paper suggests looking at the tetrahedral line complex, which is built from a tetrahedron: its six edge lines take the place of the vertices. The six edge lines and the way they are connected form the pattern of an octahedron (Figure 8), a shape known in this context as a hypersimplex. The question of the paper: can barycentric coordinates for lines be defined as ratios of canonical forms?

Later development While preparing this page (November 2026) we found no published construction of barycentric coordinates in line space; the question appears to be open. What is now known about the relevant canonical forms, and a distinction between the positive Grassmannian and the octahedron that is worth making, is summarised in Section 8.5.

Mean value coordinates

Mean value coordinates are the other widely used generalised barycentric coordinates. Unlike Wachspress coordinates they also work on non-convex polygons. They are defined through angles and distances seen from the point \mathbf x, not by rational functions (the paper calls them transcendental), so they cannot be ratios of canonical forms as defined in Section 4. They can, however, be written as Shepard-like interpolants after deforming the boundary into a circle around the current point. This suggests asking what the canonical function of a disc would be.

Strictly speaking there is none. A disc is not a positive geometry: its boundary is a single closed curve without corners, so the recursion of Section 4 never arrives at points. But there is still a natural, projectively well-defined function that is singular along a conic \mathbf x^{T}Q\,\mathbf x=0:

\Omega(\mathbb P^2,\mathcal C)=\frac{\pi\,\det(Q)^{3/4}}{(\mathbf x^{T}Q\,\mathbf x)^{3/2}}\;\omega(\mathbf x), \qquad\text{for the unit disc: } C\;\propto\;\frac{1}{(1-x^2-y^2)^{3/2}} .

Like the canonical function of a polygon, it is a dual volume: the polar dual of a disc with respect to an interior point is an ellipse, and the function is proportional to its area. But it is not rational, and it behaves differently at the boundary. At distance h from the circle it grows like 1/h^{3/2}, faster than the 1/h of a simple pole; the square root makes the singularity a branch point. This suggests looking for generalised positive geometries whose forms are not rational, on branched covers of projective space.

Later development Transforms of regions with curved boundaries that are no longer rational, including exactly this function for the disc, have since been studied; see Section 8.2. In the dual picture this function turns out to be the continuous limit of the Wachspress normaliser, while mean value coordinates correspond to a perimeter instead of an area; see Section 8.3.

Splines and integral geometry

Splines are the piecewise polynomial functions on which curve and surface design is built. The dual-volume integral at the end of Section 5 also appears in the theory of multivariate simplex, box and cone splines, where it is the Laplace transform of the indicator function of a region (the function that is one inside and zero outside). Splines and barycentric interpolants might therefore be two faces of one theory based on integral geometry.

Later development The dual-volume and Laplace-transform side of this question has been developed considerably (Section 8.2). The link to splines can be made explicit: the denominator of a canonical function is the Laplace transform of a multivariate spline, and the adjoint polynomial acts on that spline as a differential operator; see the last part of Section 8.2.

8.1 Curved boundaries: polypols, and the fall of Wachspress’ conjecture

In short: regions with curved sides also have canonical forms and adjoint curves, but a property that polygons have for free — the adjoint never vanishes inside — can fail.

The paper left out regions with curved sides for lack of space. They have since been treated systematically. Kohn, Piene, Ranestad, Rydell, Shapiro, Sinn, Sorea and Telen study polypols: planar regions bounded by arcs of algebraic curves. Wachspress’ polycons, whose sides are pieces of lines and conics, are the first case. They prove that every polypol satisfying mild conditions (quasi-regular and rational, in their terms) is a positive geometry, and that its canonical form has the same shape as for polygons in Section 6:

\Omega(P)=\frac{\operatorname{adj}_P(x,y)}{f_1(x,y)\,f_2(x,y)\cdots f_k(x,y)}\;dx\,dy .

Here f_i=0 are the equations of the boundary curves, which take the place of the distances h_i, and the numerator is an equation of the adjoint curve, scaled so that the residues at the vertices are \pm1. The adjoint plays the same role as before. The boundary curves, continued beyond the region, meet each other in further points that are not vertices (and may have singular points of their own). These are the residual points, and the adjoint passes through all of them. Its degree is d-3 when the degrees of the boundary curves add up to d. The pizza slice of Figure 3 is the simplest example: two lines and a circle give d=4, so the adjoint is a line, 1+2y=0.

Wachspress defined rational basis functions on polycons with the adjoint as common denominator. A denominator must not vanish, so these functions make sense only if the adjoint curve stays outside the element. Wachspress conjectured that for a regular polycon it always does. For convex polygons this is true (Figure 7). Kohn et al. classified the 44 topological configurations of three ellipses and proved the conjecture in 33 of them. In March 2026 Clemens Brüser settled the question in the negative: he gave an explicit regular polycon bounded by arcs of three ellipses whose adjoint cubic has a closed branch (an oval) in the interior.

What does this mean for interpolation? At a vertex where two lines meet, the wedge recipe of Section 5 still works as it stands: for the corner of the pizza slice it gives \lambda_O=(1-x^2-y^2)/(1+2y), shown in Figure 10. At vertices on a curved side something new is needed.

Wachspress’ own basis functions on a polycon

Along a straight edge a linear function is known once its values at the two end points are known; that is why two vertices per edge suffice on a polygon. Along a conic arc this is no longer true: three values are needed. Wachspress therefore puts one extra side node on every conic side; its position on the side is arbitrary. For a polycon of boundary degree m all basis functions (Wachspress calls them “wedges”) share the adjoint, of degree m-3, as denominator and have numerators of degree m-2.

The pizza slice (m=4) has four nodes: the corner O where the two straight sides meet, the two vertices V_1,V_2 on the circle, and the side node M on the arc. Write L_1,L_2 for the linear functions that vanish on the two straight sides, Q=1-x^2-y^2 for the circle and D=1+2y for the adjoint. The four basis functions are

W_O=\frac{Q}{D},\qquad W_M=k_M\frac{L_1L_2}{D},\qquad W_{V_1}=k_1\frac{L_2\,\ell_1}{D},\qquad W_{V_2}=k_2\frac{L_1\,\ell_2}{D}.

The numerators follow one rule: a basis function must vanish on every side that does not contain its node. So the corner gets Q, and the side node gets L_1L_2. A vertex where a line meets the circle gets the straight side it does not lie on, times one more linear factor \ell_k, the adjacent factor. This is the line through M and the residual point where the vertex’s own side line meets the circle a second time; it makes the function vanish at M as well. The constants k make each function equal to one at its own node. The four functions sum to one and reproduce linear functions for every position of M.

Multiplying the basis by the canonical function turns the partition of unity into a decomposition of the canonical form,

\Omega(P)=\frac{2\sqrt3\,D}{Q\,L_1L_2}\,dx\,dy =2\sqrt3\left(\frac{1}{L_1L_2}+\frac{k_M}{Q}+\frac{k_1\,\ell_1}{Q\,L_1}+\frac{k_2\,\ell_2}{Q\,L_2}\right)dx\,dy .

The first term is the wedge form of the two lines, exactly as in Section 5. The others are not canonical forms of sub-regions in any obvious way: the second has a pole only along the circle, and all three change with the arbitrary position of the side node. So on curved domains only part of Wachspress’ construction is, so far, explained by ratios of canonical forms; a full description is, to our knowledge, not in the literature. And Brüser’s example shows that positivity of the common denominator cannot be taken for granted beyond polygons.

8.2 Dual volumes, Laplace transforms and non-rational forms

In short: for a polygon the canonical function is the area of the dual polygon. For curved regions the same integral still works if the dual region is no longer counted uniformly but with a varying density.

The dual-volume formula of Section 5 has been developed in several directions that touch the second and third open problems. Three papers, in increasing distance from polygons (the technical terms in this list are explained in the papers and are not used afterwards):

  • Dual mixed volumes. Gao, Lam and Xue (2024) define a dual version of the mixed volume of several polytopes. It specialises to canonical forms and adjoints of polytopes and is additive under mixed subdivisions.
  • Canonical forms as Laplace transforms. Mazzucchelli and Raman (2025) write the canonical function of a positive geometry in projective space as the Laplace transform of a distribution on the convex dual, and call the geometry completely monotone when that distribution is a non-negative measure. Convex polytopes are of this kind. For planar regions bounded by lines and conics they compute the measures explicitly and find transcendental functions (logarithms, or equivalently inverse tangents).
  • Moment-generating functions of polypols. Shapiro (2026) studies the normalised Fantappiè transform of a planar region. For a polygon it is rational: it is the canonical form of the polar polygon. For curved polypols it is in general a branched function built from algebraic functions, logarithms and arctangents. For the unit disc it equals 2\pi\,(1-u^2-v^2)^{-3/2} — algebraic but not rational, and precisely the function plotted for the disc in Figure 9. In agreement with Section 7, that paper does not count the disc among the examples of canonical forms, because a circle has no vertices.

The second item can be seen directly. Take the polar dual P^* once and for all about the origin, not about the moving point \mathbf x. In the plane the dual-volume formula of Section 5 then reads

C(\mathbf x)=2\int_{P^*}\frac{\mu(\mathbf y)\,d\mathbf y}{(1+\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf y)^{3}},

with \mu\equiv1 for a polygon: every point of the dual counts equally, and at \mathbf x=\mathbf 0 the integral is simply the area of the dual. Now take a region with a curved side, the unit disc cut by the line y=-a. Mazzucchelli and Raman show that the same formula holds on its dual region (the unit disc together with the point dual to the cutting line, and everything between them), provided the points of the dual are counted with a density \mu between 0 and 1: \mu=\tfrac12+\tfrac1\pi\arctan\!\big((y_2-a)/\sqrt{(1-a^2)(1-|\mathbf y|^2)}\big) inside the disc and \mu=1 outside it. They prove that every such region bounded by lines and one conic is completely monotone, meaning that the density is never negative, and conjecture it for several conics.

Splines: the same transform, read backwards Added remark

In short: a canonical function is the Laplace transform of the indicator function of a cone. Splines are functions on the same cone, and their Laplace transforms are the denominators of canonical functions. The adjoint polynomial, read as a differential operator, turns the spline back into the indicator function.

The setting. Write the distance functions of a polygon in homogeneous form, h_i(\mathbf x)=\langle \mathbf a_i,(1,\mathbf x)\rangle with \mathbf a_i=(-\mathbf n_i\!\cdot\!\mathbf p_i,\;\mathbf n_i)\in\mathbb R^3. The vectors \mathbf a_0,\dots,\mathbf a_{n-1} span a cone C in three dimensions. Cutting this cone by a plane gives the dual polygon: the points (1-\mathbf x\!\cdot\!\mathbf w,\;\mathbf w) of C are those with \mathbf w\in P^*_{\mathbf x}. The Laplace transform of a function f on the cone is \mathcal L f(\mathbf u)=\int e^{-\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle}f(\mathbf v)\,d\mathbf v. In these terms the dual-volume formula of Section 5 reads

C_P(\mathbf x)=\mathcal L\big[\mathbf 1_C\big](1,\mathbf x),

the Laplace transform of the function that is one on the cone and zero outside. This is the “Laplace transform of indicator functions” that the third open problem of Section 7 refers to. The cone does not depend on \mathbf x; the point \mathbf x only chooses the plane that cuts it, and the transform is 3! times the volume of the pyramid that this plane cuts off.

The spline. There is a second natural function on the same cone. Send the positive part of n-dimensional space to the cone by (t_0,\dots,t_{n-1})\mapsto\sum_i t_i\,\mathbf a_i. For n>3 many points \mathbf t land on the same point \mathbf v; they form a polytope of dimension n-3, and its volume (suitably normalised) is a function T(\mathbf v) on the cone. This is the multivariate spline, or truncated power, of the vectors \mathbf a_i: the density of the shadow that the positive orthant casts on the cone. The classical facts about it (De Concini and Procesi, Chapter 7) are:

  • it is piecewise polynomial and homogeneous of degree n-3; its formula changes across the planes through two of the vectors \mathbf a_i, which cut the dual polygon along its diagonals;
  • for n\ge4 it is continuous and vanishes on the boundary of the cone; if no three of the vectors lie in a plane (no three edge lines of the polygon pass through one point), it has n-4 continuous derivatives;
  • the derivative in the direction \mathbf a_k is the spline of the remaining vectors, D_{\mathbf a_k}T_{\{\mathbf a_i\}}=T_{\{\mathbf a_i\}\setminus\mathbf a_k}, and for three vectors the spline is the indicator function of their cone divided by |\det|;
  • its Laplace transform is 1/\prod_i\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf a_i\rangle.

The last item is the link. Evaluated at \mathbf u=(1,\mathbf x), the two functions on the cone give the two halves of the canonical function:

\mathcal L\big[\mathbf 1_C\big](1,\mathbf x)=\frac{\operatorname{adj}_P(\mathbf x)}{\prod_i h_i(\mathbf x)}, \qquad\qquad \mathcal L\big[T\big](1,\mathbf x)=\frac{1}{\prod_i h_i(\mathbf x)} .

On the dual polygon, where \tau_{\mathbf x}(\mathbf w)=T(1-\mathbf x\!\cdot\!\mathbf w,\mathbf w) is the spline restricted to the slice, the same two statements are

C_P(\mathbf x)=2\operatorname{Area}(P^*_{\mathbf x}), \qquad\qquad \frac{1}{\prod_i h_i(\mathbf x)}=(n-1)!\int_{P^*_{\mathbf x}}\tau_{\mathbf x}(\mathbf w)\,d\mathbf w .

So the adjoint polynomial at \mathbf x is the ratio of two measurements of one dual polygon: twice its area, and (n-1)! times the mass of the spline on it.

The adjoint as a differential operator. Under the Laplace transform, multiplying by the linear function \langle\mathbf u,\mathbf a\rangle corresponds to differentiating in the direction \mathbf a. A polynomial in \mathbf u therefore corresponds to a differential operator with constant coefficients. Write the adjoint, in homogeneous form, by Warren’s formula for a triangulation of the dual polygon into triangles \sigma:

\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf u)=\sum_{\sigma}|\det\sigma|\prod_{k\notin\sigma}\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf a_k\rangle \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \operatorname{adj}(\partial)\,T=\sum_{\sigma}|\det\sigma|\prod_{k\notin\sigma}D_{\mathbf a_k}T =\sum_\sigma \mathbf 1_{\operatorname{cone}(\sigma)}=\mathbf 1_C .

Each product of derivatives removes all vectors except the three of \sigma, and what is left is the indicator function of one triangular cone; the triangular cones tile C. In words: the spline is a tent-like function on the dual polygon, built from polynomials of degree n-3, and the adjoint is the differential operator of the same order n-3 that flattens it to the constant one. This gives the adjoint a meaning that does not refer to poles or to curves through intersection points.

The rule behind the operator formula, that a derivative along one of the vectors removes that vector, can be tried out directly. Each removal lowers the degree of the spline by one and shrinks its support to the cone of the remaining vectors. After n-3 removals three vectors are left, and the spline has become the indicator function of a triangular cone divided by a determinant. The adjoint operator is the sum of such complete sequences of removals, one for each triangle of a triangulation of the dual polygon, weighted by the determinants.

The row on linear precision deserves a comment. The term w_i\,(1,\mathbf p_i) is the Laplace transform of the two-dimensional face of the cone spanned by \mathbf a_{i-1} and \mathbf a_i, multiplied by the unit normal of that face, which points along the homogeneous vertex (1,\mathbf p_i). Stokes’ theorem for indicator functions of polytopes is the set of “standard relations” from which Tveiten builds the D-module of a polytope; he shows that, after projection, these relations correspond to the recurrence relations of B-splines found by de Boor and Höllig. Linear precision of Wachspress coordinates and the recurrence for B-splines are thus two images of one identity.

One variable: B-splines and the barycentric formula. The simplest case connects with Section 1. Take n+1 nodes t_0<\dots<t_n on a line. Polynomial interpolation at these nodes can be written in the Shepard-like form of Section 1 (the barycentric formula; see Hormann’s survey),

f(x)=\frac{\sum_i \dfrac{c_i}{x-t_i}\,f(t_i)}{\sum_i \dfrac{c_i}{x-t_i}},\qquad c_i=\prod_{j\ne i}\frac{1}{t_i-t_j},\qquad \sum_i\frac{c_i}{x-t_i}=\frac{1}{\prod_i (x-t_i)} .

The denominator has a pole at every node. On the spline side stands the B-spline M(y) with the nodes as knots: by a theorem of Curry and Schoenberg it is the density of the shadow of an n-dimensional simplex on a line, the knots being the shadows of the vertices. For x outside the interval of the nodes the two are related by

\int M(y)\,\frac{dy}{(x-y)^{n+1}}=\frac{1}{\prod_{i=0}^{n}(x-t_i)} ,

the one-variable version of the formula for 1/\prod h_i above. For two nodes the B-spline is the uniform density on [a,b] and the right-hand side is, up to a constant factor, the canonical function of the segment from Section 1. For more nodes the weights c_i alternate in sign, which is why Lagrange’s basis functions are not positive: the function 1/\prod(x-t_i) is the transform of a positive spline, but it is a canonical function only in the case of two nodes.

What is known, and what the open problem looks like now. The spline facts are classical. The link with rational functions through the Laplace transform is the organising idea of De Concini and Procesi’s book, which studies the algebra of rational functions with poles on a hyperplane arrangement as a module over differential operators; the canonical forms of the regions of an arrangement live in the cohomology of its complement (Eur and Lam). In physics, Paulos observed in 2012 that one-loop integrands are Laplace transforms of splines and that numerators act on the spline as differential operators. Kohn, Shapiro and Sturmfels show that the adjoint is the numerator of the moment generating function of the uniform measure on a polytope, and call the densities of its projections polytopal splines. Gao, Lam and Xue have begun a discrete version, with lattice points in place of volumes; on the spline side its counterpart is the theory of partition functions in the second half of De Concini and Procesi’s book.

So, at the level of this dictionary, the third open problem has a positive answer: barycentric coordinates and splines are obtained from the same objects, indicator functions of cones and their shadows, on the two sides of the Laplace transform. What the dictionary does not yet provide is a use of one theory for the other. The spline lives on the dual polygon, not on the polygon where one interpolates, and the parts of spline theory that matter in approximation (translates on a grid, refinement, approximation order) have no counterpart for canonical forms so far.

The statement that the adjoint, as a differential operator, turns the spline into the indicator function, the formula with (n-1)!, and the reading of linear precision as Stokes’ theorem are elementary consequences of the cited facts. They are checked in the project’s tests; we did not find them stated in this form.

Toric varieties and symplectic geometry: the same objects once more Added remark

In short: both functions on the dual cone are measures of the kind studied by Duistermaat and Heckman. The spline belongs to a torus acting on \mathbb C^n, the indicator function to a toric cone built from the polygon. The canonical function is then a volume that geometers and physicists minimise, and the Wachspress coordinates of the minimising point are known there under another name.

Background: moment maps and the Duistermaat–Heckman theorem, in one paragraph

When a torus acts on a phase space of mechanics (a symplectic manifold) and preserves its structure, the conserved quantities of the action fit together into a moment map from the manifold to a real vector space; its image is convex. Pushing the natural volume of the manifold forward along the moment map gives a measure on that vector space, the Duistermaat–Heckman measure. Their theorem says that its density is piecewise polynomial, and that its Laplace transform is given exactly by a sum over the fixed points of the action: each fixed point contributes an exponential divided by the product of the weights with which the torus acts near it. The density at a point is the volume of the reduced space over that point (the fibre of the moment map, divided by the torus). For a toric variety, where the torus has half the dimension of the manifold, the image is a polytope and the measure is uniform on it.

The spline is a Duistermaat–Heckman measure. Let a three-dimensional torus act on \mathbb C^n by rotating the k-th coordinate with weight \mathbf a_k (this needs integer vectors; the statements about measures and polytopes below hold for any polygon). Its moment map is \mathbf z\mapsto\sum_k \tfrac12|z_k|^2\,\mathbf a_k. With t_k=\tfrac12|z_k|^2 this is the map \mathbf t\mapsto\sum_k t_k\mathbf a_k that defined the spline, so the Duistermaat–Heckman measure of this action is the spline, up to the factor (2\pi)^n. The action has a single fixed point, the origin, with weights \mathbf a_0,\dots,\mathbf a_{n-1}, and the fixed-point formula gives 1/\prod_k\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf a_k\rangle: the Laplace transform of the spline is the simplest instance of the theorem. The box spline is the same construction with a product of n spheres in place of \mathbb C^n; its 2^n fixed points give the 2^n terms of \prod_k(1-e^{-\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf a_k\rangle})/\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf a_k\rangle.

The value of the spline at a point \mathbf v of the cone is the volume of the reduced space over \mathbf v. That space is itself a toric variety, of complex dimension n-3, and its moment polytope is the fibre \{\mathbf t\ge 0:\ \sum_k t_k\mathbf a_k=\mathbf v\} from the definition of the spline. When \mathbf v crosses a diagonal of the dual polygon, this polytope changes its shape (for a pentagon, one of its sides disappears) and with it the reduced space; the diagonals are the walls of the variation of symplectic quotients, and this is the reason why the spline changes its formula there.

The indicator function is one too, and its transform is a volume. Suppose the vertices of P have integer coordinates. The cone over P then defines an affine toric variety Y of complex dimension three, whose polynomial functions are spanned by the monomials with exponents in the lattice points of the dual cone C. The moment map of Y has image C, and since Y is toric its Duistermaat–Heckman measure is uniform: it is the indicator function \mathbf 1_C. So the canonical function C_P(\mathbf x), its Laplace transform at (1,\mathbf x), is a volume attached to Y and to the direction (1,\mathbf x) in the torus: up to 3!, the volume of the pyramid of Figure 12b. Counting the monomials on Y with a weight gives the same function in the limit,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to0}\ \varepsilon^{3}\!\!\sum_{\mathbf m\in C\cap\mathbb Z^3}\! e^{-\varepsilon\langle(1,\mathbf x),\mathbf m\rangle}=C_P(\mathbf x) ,

which is how a canonical function arises from a count of lattice points.

This volume has been studied intensively for a different reason. Martelli, Sparks and Yau call P the toric diagram of Y, a Calabi–Yau cone, and consider metrics on its five-dimensional base that are determined by a Reeb vector \mathbf b in the interior of the cone over P. They show that the volume of the base depends only on \mathbf b, and for \mathbf b=3\,(1,\mathbf x) their formula is, in the notation of this page,

\operatorname{vol}(\mathbf b)=\frac{\pi^3}{b_1}\sum_a\frac{(\mathbf v_{a-1},\mathbf v_a,\mathbf v_{a+1})}{(\mathbf b,\mathbf v_{a-1},\mathbf v_a)\,(\mathbf b,\mathbf v_a,\mathbf v_{a+1})} =\frac{\pi^3}{27}\,C_P(\mathbf x),\qquad \mathbf v_a=(1,\mathbf p_a),

with one term for every vertex: the sum of the wedge weights of Section 5. They prove that this function is strictly convex and infinite on the boundary, so that it has exactly one minimum, and that the minimising Reeb vector is the one of a Sasaki–Einstein metric, if there is one (existence was shown by Futaki, Ono and Wang, and in greater generality by Berman). Three remarks translate this into the language of this page.

  • The minimiser is the Santaló point. Since C_P(\mathbf x) is twice the area of the dual polygon about \mathbf x, minimising the volume means finding the point about which the dual polygon is smallest. Convex geometry calls it the Santaló point of P; it is the point about which the dual polygon has its centroid at the origin.
  • The wedge weights are volumes. In the same paper the weight w_a is, up to a constant, the volume of the three-dimensional submanifold of the base that belongs to the vertex \mathbf p_a.
  • Wachspress coordinates are R-charges. In the gauge theories that physicists attach to Y, every vertex of the toric diagram carries a number, its R-charge, and the charges sum to two. Butti and Zaffaroni found that they are 2\,l_a/\sum_b l_b with l_a, up to a constant, the corner area divided by the product of the two triangle areas at the vertex. In the notation of Section 3 this says that the R-charges are twice the Wachspress coordinates of the Reeb point, and their “vectorial identity” is linear precision.

The known volumes are reproduced by minimising C_P/27: 16/27 for the unit square (the conifold), (46+13\sqrt{13})/324 and (59+11\sqrt{33})/486 for the quadrilateral and the pentagon of the first two del Pezzo surfaces, in units of \pi^3.

The adjoint as an equivariant class. The two measures belong to two spaces, one inside the other. For integer vectors \mathbf a_k, the torus orbit of the point (1,\dots,1) in \mathbb C^n closes up to a three-dimensional toric variety Y_A with moment cone C. The volume function of \mathbb C^n is 1/\prod\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf a_k\rangle, that of Y_A is \operatorname{adj}(\mathbf u)/\prod\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf a_k\rangle, and their ratio, the adjoint, is the class of Y_A in the equivariant cohomology of \mathbb C^n, which commutative algebra calls its multidegree. The multidegree of a toric variety can be computed from a triangulation, through Sturmfels’ description of the initial ideals of toric ideals and the additivity of multidegrees, and the result is Warren’s formula for the adjoint. For a quadrilateral the four vectors satisfy one relation c_0\mathbf a_0+c_2\mathbf a_2=c_1\mathbf a_1+c_3\mathbf a_3 with positive coefficients, Y_A is the hypersurface z_0^{c_0}z_2^{c_2}=z_1^{c_1}z_3^{c_3}, and its class \langle\mathbf u,c_0\mathbf a_0+c_2\mathbf a_2\rangle is, up to a constant factor, the adjoint line c_0h_0+c_2h_2; for the unit square this is the conifold z_0z_2=z_1z_3.

The polygon as a quotient, and two kinds of push-forward. The polygon is itself a moment polytope, of a toric surface (for suitable lattice polygons; Delzant). In Delzant’s construction that surface is a quotient of \mathbb C^n, and the functions \tfrac12|z_k|^2 become the distances h_k(\mathbf x), up to the normalisation of the edge normals: the embedding of the polygon into the positive orthant by its distance functions, used in the dictionary above, is the moment-map picture of the homogeneous coordinates of a toric variety. The push-forward of Section 8.4 lives here as well. Arkani-Hamed, Bai and Lam obtain the canonical form of a polytope by pushing the form \prod dX_i/X_i of a toric variety forward along a map of the same shape as the moment map, and call the result an algebraic analogue of the Duistermaat–Heckman measure. The difference is in the kind of push-forward. The measure is obtained by integrating a volume over the fibres; the canonical form by summing a rational form over finitely many preimages.

Newton polytopes. For a polynomial p with positive coefficients, the polytope spanned by its exponents is its Newton polytope N. Arkani-Hamed, He and Lam consider the integral

I(\mathbf X)=\alpha'^{\,d}\int_{\mathbb R^d_{>0}}\prod_i\frac{dx_i}{x_i}\;\mathbf x^{\alpha'\mathbf X}\,p(\mathbf x)^{-\alpha' c},

which converges when \mathbf X lies inside cN, and show that for \alpha'\to0 it tends to the canonical function of cN at \mathbf X. The simplest case is p=1+x: the integral is a Beta function, and its limit 1/X+1/(c-X) is the function of Section 1. The coefficients of p do not matter in the limit, only its Newton polytope. The points where the integrand is stationary satisfy \mathbf X=c\,\partial\log p/\partial\log\mathbf x; this is a map from the positive orthant onto the interior of the polytope of the same kind as a moment map, and summing over its preimages is the push-forward just described. Here a Mellin transform plays the role that the Laplace transform played above.

The translations into the notation of this page — the volume of Martelli, Sparks and Yau as the canonical function divided by 27, its minimiser as the Santaló point, the R-charges as twice the Wachspress coordinates, the adjoint as a multidegree — are direct comparisons of formulas. One variable again: the barycentric formula, residues and contour integrals Added remark

In short: with more than two nodes on a line, the formula of Section 1 becomes the barycentric formula of approximation theory. It is the simplex formula of Section 2 read along a curve. Its properties follow from the residue theorem, and Hermite’s contour integral gives its error. Positivity is lost beyond two nodes; what survives of the canonical form is the behaviour at the poles and at infinity.

The formula. Take nodes t_0<\dots<t_n and their node polynomial \ell(x)=\prod_j(x-t_j). The barycentric formula displayed above Figure 12e says that the polynomial of degree at most n through the data is \sum_j\lambda_j(x)f(t_j) with

\lambda_j(x)=\frac{c_j}{x-t_j}\Big/\sum_k\frac{c_k}{x-t_k},\qquad c_j=\frac{1}{\ell'(t_j)},\qquad \sum_k\frac{c_k}{x-t_k}=\frac{1}{\ell(x)} .

The weight c_j is the residue of 1/\ell at the node t_j, and the last identity is the partial fraction expansion of 1/\ell. For two nodes a,b this is Section 1: there 1/\ell equals -1/(b-a) times the canonical function of the segment. Trefethen’s book, which we follow in this part, calls it the second form of the barycentric interpolation formula and traces its origins to Jacobi’s thesis of 1825.

A simplex on a curve. Why should a formula with poles produce a polynomial? Lift every node to the point (t_j,t_j^2,\dots,t_j^n) of the moment curve in n dimensions. The n+1 lifted nodes are the vertices of a simplex, and the barycentric coordinates of the point (x,x^2,\dots,x^n) with respect to this simplex are exactly the \lambda_j(x): both sets of numbers sum to one and reproduce x,x^2,\dots,x^n, and these conditions determine them. The facet opposite the vertex j passes through the other n lifted nodes, so along the curve its equation is \prod_{k\ne j}(x-t_k), up to a constant factor. Now apply the recipe of Section 2, in which the weight of a vertex is one over the product of the volumes A_k that belong to the other vertices, and so has its poles on the facets through the vertex:

\frac{1}{\prod_{k\ne j}A_k}\;\propto\;\frac{c_j}{(x-t_j)\,\ell(x)^{\,n-1}} \qquad\text{and}\qquad C_{\text{simplex}}\big(x,x^2,\dots,x^n\big)=\pm\frac{V}{\ell(x)^{\,n}},

where V=\prod_{i<k}(t_k-t_i) is n! times the volume of the simplex and the sign is (-1)^{n(n+1)/2}. The common factor \ell^{\,n-1} cancels in the ratio, and what remains is the barycentric formula. So the formula of approximation theory is the formula of Section 2, restricted to a curve. The picture also shows what goes wrong. For more than two nodes the curve never enters the simplex: it touches it at the vertices only. A point outside a simplex has a negative coordinate, and this is why Lagrange’s basis polynomials change sign.

Partition of unity from the residue theorem. Fix x, take a polynomial g, and look at the differential form

\omega=\frac{\ell(x)}{\ell(t)}\,\frac{g(t)\,dt}{x-t}

in the variable t. It has a pole at every node, with residue \lambda_j(x)\,g(t_j), and a pole at t=x with residue -g(x). If the degree of g is at most n, it has no pole at infinity. On the Riemann sphere the residues of a rational form add up to zero, hence

\sum_j\lambda_j(x)\,g(t_j)=g(x)\qquad\text{for every polynomial } g \text{ of degree at most } n .

With g=1 this is the partition of unity, with g(t)=t linear precision. The argument has the same shape as the first answer in Section 5: the weights sum to the right thing because nothing is left over at infinity.

Other weights, and a one-variable adjoint. Replace the c_j by any non-zero numbers w_j and write R(x)=\sum_j w_j/(x-t_j). The quotient \sum_j w_jf(t_j)/(x-t_j)\,\big/\,R(x) still takes the value f(t_j) at every node, for the reason given in Section 1, but it is now a rational function. The residue argument goes through with R(t)/R(x) in place of \ell(x)/\ell(t), and it shows what the weights decide:

  • Constants are always reproduced. Polynomials of degree up to d are reproduced exactly when R decays at least as fast as t^{-d-1} at infinity, that is, when \sum_j w_jt_j^{\,k}=0 for k=0,\dots,d-1. In particular the coordinates have linear precision exactly when \sum_jw_j=0, which says that the form R\,dt has no pole at infinity.
  • Clearing the poles gives a polynomial D(x)=\ell(x)R(x)=\sum_jw_j\prod_{k\ne j}(x-t_k) of degree n-d (for the largest such d), and \lambda_j=w_j\prod_{k\ne j}(x-t_k)/D(x). The zeros of D are the poles of the interpolant. D is constant exactly for the weights c_j, the polynomial case.

The last row is elementary. For x between two neighbouring nodes all coordinates are non-negative only if the weights of the nodes to the left of x have one sign and those to the right the other. That cannot hold in every gap unless there are just two nodes. So with three or more nodes some coordinate is negative somewhere, whatever the weights. Approximation theory measures the damage by the Lebesgue function \sum_j|\lambda_j(x)|. It equals one exactly where no coordinate is negative, as for Wachspress coordinates inside a polygon; for polynomial interpolation at equally spaced nodes its maximum grows exponentially with the number of nodes.

A canonical form after all: Berrut’s interpolant. On a line, the positive geometries are the finite unions of disjoint intervals, and the canonical form of such a union has residues +1 and -1 in turn at the end points. For an even number of nodes this is the form R\,dt with the weights w_j=(-1)^j:

\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^j}{x-t_j}=C_A(x),\qquad A=[t_0,t_1]\cup[t_2,t_3]\cup\dots\cup[t_{n-1},t_n].

These are the weights of Berrut’s rational interpolant. In the language of this page its coordinates are ratios of canonical functions, a half-line over a union of intervals, and its two known properties can be read off. The weights sum to zero, so the coordinates have linear precision. And C_A has no zero on the real line (for x in a given gap, group the terms of the other nodes in neighbouring pairs, starting next to the gap: every pair has the sign of the two terms nearest to x), so the interpolant has no real poles, as Berrut showed. With the weights c_j of polynomial interpolation, by contrast, the residues of dt/\ell are not \pm1, and the form is a canonical form only for two nodes. Floater and Hormann found a family in between: for each d, weights without real poles that reproduce polynomials of degree d; d=0 is Berrut’s interpolant and d=n the polynomial.

Hermite’s formula. Now let f be analytic inside a closed curve \Gamma around the nodes and the point x. The form of the residue argument, with f in place of the polynomial g, has its only poles inside \Gamma at the nodes and at x, and the residue theorem turns into a formula for the error of the interpolating polynomial p:

f(x)-p(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_\Gamma\frac{\ell(x)}{\ell(t)}\,\frac{f(t)}{t-x}\,dt .

This is the Hermite integral formula; without the factor \ell(x)/\ell(t) it is Cauchy’s integral formula. For other weights the same holds with R(t)/R(x) as the factor. In particular, for the two nodes of Section 1 the factor is a ratio of canonical functions of the segment, C_{[a,b]}(t)/C_{[a,b]}(x).

The formula is used to see where interpolation works. Everything depends on the size of \ell(x), at the point of interest, compared with \ell(t) on a curve inside which f is analytic. Since \log|\ell(z)|=\sum_j\log|z-t_j| is the potential of equal charges placed at the nodes, this is a question of potential theory. If f is itself rational, with simple poles \pi_k and residues \rho_k, and vanishes at infinity, the contour can be moved outwards until only the poles of f contribute:

f(x)-p(x)=\sum_k\rho_k\,\frac{\ell(x)}{\ell(\pi_k)\,(x-\pi_k)} .

The error at x is small where the potential at x is lower than at the poles, and grows with the number of nodes where it is higher. For equally spaced nodes the potential is lowest in the middle of the interval. For Runge’s function 1/(1+25x^2), with poles at \pm i/5, the interpolants therefore converge only for |x|<0.7266\ldots and diverge nearer the ends. For Chebyshev nodes the limiting potential is constant on the interval, its level curves are ellipses with foci \pm1, and the interpolants converge on the whole interval for every function analytic in a neighbourhood of it.

The real twin: divided differences and B-splines. The coefficients c_j also define the divided difference f[t_0,\dots,t_n]=\sum_jc_jf(t_j), and this number has a complex and a real integral formula,

f[t_0,\dots,t_n]=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f(t)}{\ell(t)}\,dt =\frac{1}{n!}\int M(y)\,f^{(n)}(y)\,dy ,

where M is the B-spline of Figure 12e. The two kernels, 1/\ell and M, are related by the transform displayed above that figure. The error of interpolation is \ell(x) times a divided difference with x as one more node, so Hermite’s formula has a real twin as well:

f(x)-p(x)=\frac{\ell(x)}{(n+1)!}\int M(y\mid t_0,\dots,t_n,x)\,f^{(n+1)}(y)\,dy .

The evaluation point has become a knot of the spline. For the two nodes of Section 1 the spline is the hat function with knots a,x,b, and the formula reads f(x)-p(x)=-\frac{b-a}{2\,C_{[a,b]}(x)}\int M(y\mid a,x,b)\,f''(y)\,dy. In one variable, then, the splines of the third open problem are the real kernels of the contour formulas of interpolation.

Contour integrals for canonical forms. Positive geometry has contour formulas of its own. Arkani-Hamed, Bai and Lam write the canonical function of a polytope with vertices Z_1,\dots,Z_n as an integral over real variables c_j, one for each vertex, with a pole at each c_j=0 (moved slightly off the real axis) and the constraint Y=\sum_jc_jZ_j. The residue at which all but (dimension + 1) of the c_j vanish is the canonical function of the simplex on the remaining vertices. For a quadrilateral, closing the contour on one side or the other produces its two triangulations, and the authors conclude that the independence of the canonical function from the triangulation is “a consequence of Cauchy’s theorem”. A second formula of theirs uses the residue theorem in several variables for the same purpose. They also note that the formula 1/\prod_iA_i=m!\int d^m\alpha\,/(A_0+\sum_i\alpha_iA_i)^{m+1}, known in physics as Feynman parametrisation, is the dual-volume formula for a simplex; in one variable it is the transform above Figure 12e. Shapiro (2026) shows that the same kind of transform of a polygon, which he calls a Fantappiè transform, is the canonical form of the polar polygon.

These formulas concern the canonical form itself. What we did not find is a counterpart of Hermite’s formula for a polygon: a kernel in two complex variables, built from the canonical form, whose residues at the vertices are the Wachspress coordinates, and which would give the error of Wachspress interpolation for analytic functions. On the side of interpolation, Hormann’s survey treats rational interpolation on a line and coordinates on polygons under the common name “barycentric”, as blends of local linear interpolants; Floater and Hormann describe their interpolant as a one-variable analogue of a construction in the plane.

The barycentric formula, Hermite’s formula, the potential theory and the numbers for Runge’s example are taken from Trefethen’s book; the rational interpolants are those of Berrut and of Floater and Hormann; the formulas for divided differences are in de Boor’s survey. The following are elementary observations of ours, checked in the project’s tests, which we did not find stated in this form: the barycentric formula as the simplex formula on the moment curve, with \pm V/\ell^{\,n} as the canonical function; the criterion for the degree of precision as a statement about the pole at infinity (Hormann’s survey states that \sum_jw_j=0 gives linear precision); the comparison of the denominator with the adjoint; and the denominator of Berrut’s interpolant as the canonical function of a union of intervals.

8.3 Mean value coordinates in the dual picture: area versus perimeter

In short: Wachspress and mean value coordinates measure the same dual polygon in two ways. Wachspress weights add up to twice its area, mean value weights to its perimeter.

Section 7 related the disc function (eq. (5.14) of the paper, the function plotted in Figure 9) to mean value coordinates. The dual picture of Section 5 allows a sharper statement. Schaefer, Ju and Warren showed that Wachspress and mean value coordinates are two members of one family of dual Shepard interpolants. Recall that the vertex \mathbf p_i corresponds to one edge of the dual polygon P^*_{\mathbf x}. Write L_i for the length of that edge and r_i=|\mathbf p_i-\mathbf x| for the distance from \mathbf x to the vertex. Then the normalising sums of the two kinds of weights are

\underbrace{\sum_i w_i^{\mathrm{W}}}_{\text{Wachspress}}=\sum_i\frac{L_i}{r_i}=2\operatorname{Area}(P^*_{\mathbf x}), \qquad\qquad \underbrace{\sum_i w_i^{\mathrm{MV}}}_{\text{mean value}}=\sum_i L_i=\operatorname{Perimeter}(P^*_{\mathbf x}).

For Wachspress coordinates the statement holds weight by weight, w_i=L_i/r_i: the dual edge lies at distance 1/r_i from the origin, so L_i/r_i is twice the area of the triangle over that edge, as in Section 5. Linear precision becomes the fact that the edge vectors of a closed polygon sum to zero. For mean value coordinates only the total is the perimeter; the individual weights are distributed differently.

In the mechanical reading of Section 5, every set of barycentric coordinates has a closed force polygon at the point \mathbf x: its edge i is perpendicular to the direction from \mathbf x to \mathbf p_i and has length w_i\,r_i. Discrete harmonic coordinates are the “cotangent weights” of geometry processing; they are rational but can be negative. The three are the members p=0,1,2 of the family of Floater, Hormann and Kós. The table shows at a glance why mean value coordinates are not rational: the line carrying edge i is \mathbf y\cdot(\mathbf p_i-\mathbf x)=c with c=1 for Wachspress and c=r_i^2 for discrete harmonic coordinates, but c=r_i, a square root, for mean value coordinates. For the unit disc and a point at distance r from the centre, the dual is an ellipse with semi-axes 1/(1-r^2) and 1/\sqrt{1-r^2}, so the two normalisers are

2\operatorname{Area}=\frac{2\pi}{(1-r^2)^{3/2}},\qquad \operatorname{Perimeter}=\frac{4\,E(r)}{1-r^2},\qquad E(r)=\int_0^{\pi/2}\!\sqrt{1-r^2\sin^2\varphi}\;d\varphi .

The first is the function of eq. (5.14) and Figure 9. It is therefore the continuous limit of the Wachspress normaliser. The mean value analogue is the second: an elliptic integral, which grows only like 2/h at distance h from the boundary.

The same limit can be taken for the coordinates themselves. Warren, Schaefer, Hirani and Desbrun extended Wachspress coordinates to smooth convex sets, with a weight \kappa(\mathbf v)/\big(\mathbf n(\mathbf v)\cdot(\mathbf v-\mathbf x)\big)^{d} at the boundary point \mathbf v (curvature over a power of the distance to the tangent plane). For the unit circle this is 1/(1-\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf x)^2, and its integral over the circle is again 2\pi/(1-|\mathbf x|^2)^{3/2}.

The area/perimeter comparison, the elliptic-integral formula and the identification of eq. (5.14) with the Wachspress-type kernel are observations made while preparing this page. They are verified numerically in the project’s tests, but we did not find them stated in the literature, and a positive-geometry description of mean value coordinates themselves remains open.

8.4 Push-forwards, bilinear patches and polynomial coordinates

In short: the canonical function of a complicated region can be computed from that of a simple one, if a map carries the simple region onto the complicated one. For a quadrilateral the map is the bilinear patch.

Besides triangulation there is a second way to compute canonical forms, already in Arkani-Hamed, Bai and Lam: the push-forward. Suppose a map sends one positive geometry onto another. To find the canonical function of the image at a point \mathbf x, collect all points that the map sends to \mathbf x (its preimages), evaluate the canonical function of the source at each of them, divide by the Jacobian determinant of the map there (the factor by which it stretches areas), and add up.

For a quadrilateral the map is one every modeller knows: the bilinear patch B(u,v), which carries the unit square onto the quadrilateral. The square is a product of two segments, so its canonical function is the product of two segment formulas, 1/\big(u(1-u)\,v(1-v)\big), and the recipe gives

C_{\text{quad}}(\mathbf x)=\sum_{B(u,v)=\mathbf x}\frac{1}{u(1-u)\,v(1-v)\,\det\!\big(\partial_uB,\partial_vB\big)} .

How many terms does the sum have? Solving B(u,v)=\mathbf x for (u,v), known as inverse bilinear interpolation, leads to a quadratic equation, so in general there are two solutions (for a parallelogram, only one). For a point \mathbf x of the quadrilateral one solution lies in the square and the other outside it, and it is tempting to discard the second. It must be kept: each term alone contains a square root, and only their sum is a rational function of \mathbf x. In the same way the canonical form of any polygon is the push-forward of \tfrac{dX_1}{X_1}\wedge\tfrac{dX_2}{X_2} under a suitable toric patch, a generalisation of Bézier patches whose domain is a polygon.

The bilinear patch also gives barycentric coordinates directly. Its four blending functions (1-u)(1-v),\,u(1-v),\,uv,\,(1-u)v, evaluated at the preimage inside the square, are non-negative, sum to one and reproduce \mathbf x. But as functions of \mathbf x they contain the square root from the quadratic equation, and in general they differ from the Wachspress coordinates. For a parallelogram the map is affine, the two sets of coordinates coincide, the adjoint is constant and the coordinates are polynomials.

When are barycentric coordinates polynomials? Wachspress already observed that for a quadrilateral this happens only if it is a parallelogram. Baumbach and Winter (2026) settle the general case: a polytope admits polynomial generalised barycentric coordinates if and only if it is, up to an affine map, a product of simplices (such as a parallelogram or a prism), and Wachspress coordinates are the unique rational coordinates of minimal degree. For toric patches, Garcia-Puente and Sottile relate linear precision to a property from algebraic statistics, maximum likelihood degree one.

8.5 Line space: what is known about the canonical form

In short: the positive part of line space has only four boundary pieces and less symmetry than the octahedron of Figure 8; the octahedron is a three-dimensional shadow of it. A first “adjoint” in line space has been found.

The first open problem asks for barycentric coordinates in the space of lines (see Section 7 for Plücker coordinates p_{ij}). No such construction has been published, but the ingredients are now explicit, and they suggest keeping two objects apart: the positive Grassmannian itself, and the octahedron.

  • The positive Grassmannian has four facets. Its canonical form has poles exactly where one of the cyclically consecutive Plücker coordinates p_{12},p_{23},p_{34},p_{14} vanishes (Arkani-Hamed, Bai, Lam, eq. 5.40). To write it down one needs four free parameters for a line. For G(2,4), take the lines spanned by the two rows of \left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&-c&-d\\0&1&a&b\end{smallmatrix}\right), with parameters a,b,c,d. This gives
    \Omega\big(G_{\ge0}(2,4)\big)=\frac{da\,db\,dc\,dd}{b\,c\,(ad-bc)},\qquad p_{12}=1,\;p_{23}=c,\;p_{34}=ad-bc,\;p_{14}=b .
    The denominator contains exactly the consecutive coordinates. The other two, p_{13}=a and p_{24}=d, cannot vanish in the interior, because the Plücker equation says p_{13}p_{24}=p_{12}p_{34}+p_{14}p_{23}, a sum of positive numbers. So the six edge lines of the tetrahedron are not treated alike: this positive geometry has a cyclic symmetry, not the full symmetry of a tetrahedron.
  • The octahedron is a moment-map image. Divide the six Plücker coordinates by their sum. The six numbers p_{ij}/\sum p_{kl} are non-negative and sum to one, and using them as weights for the six vertices of the octahedron of Figure 8 maps the positive Grassmannian onto that octahedron. They look like barycentric coordinates with respect to the six edge lines. But the positive Grassmannian is four-dimensional and the octahedron three-dimensional, so different lines land on the same point: a point of the octahedron does not determine a line; Łukowski and Stalknecht describe the canonical forms that arise on the octahedron.
  • A first adjoint in line space. Pavlov and Ranestad (2025) cut the positive Grassmannian with one more hyperplane \sum c_{ij}p_{ij}\ge0 and show that, under genericity assumptions, the resulting “positive pentahedron” is a positive geometry with a unique adjoint, the linear form \sum\max(c_{ij},0)\,p_{ij}. Loosely speaking, this is the step from a triangle to a quadrilateral.

The chart formula above is our own evaluation of eq. (5.40). Which of these objects should play the role of the “simplex” for interpolation, and what replaces linear precision, is open.
