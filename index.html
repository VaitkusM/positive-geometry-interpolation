Positive Geometries for Barycentric Interpolation

after Márton Vaitkus, Budapest University of Technology and Economics

Linear interpolation on a segment, barycentric coordinates on a triangle and Wachspress coordinates on a convex polygon are all ratios of rational functions whose poles sit on the boundary of the domain. Positive geometries, a notion from theoretical physics, explain where these functions come from and why the recipe works. All figures on this page are interactive.

Sections 1–7 follow the 2019 paper. Material marked Later development reports results published since then, and Added illustration marks figures that go beyond the paper’s own.

Two ways into this page

Coming from geometric modelling or graphics? You know barycentric coordinates; Sections 1–3 only rewrite them in an unusual way. The new material is in Section 4, where the grey “background” boxes introduce the few notions needed about differential forms and residues.

Coming from pure mathematics or physics? You may know forms, residues and perhaps the amplituhedron. Sections 1–3 explain what interpolation people want from “barycentric coordinates”; Section 5 shows that canonical forms deliver exactly that.

1 Interpolation on a segment, written with poles

Given values f(a) and f(b) at the two ends of a segment, linear interpolation fills in the values in between:

f(x)=\underbrace{\frac{b-x}{b-a}}_{\lambda_a(x)}\,f(a)+\underbrace{\frac{x-a}{b-a}}_{\lambda_b(x)}\,f(b).

The weights \lambda_a,\lambda_b are the barycentric coordinates of x: they are positive inside the segment, sum to one, and \lambda_a=1 at a. Dividing numerator and denominator by (x-a)(b-x) gives the same function in barycentric form:

f(x)=\frac{\dfrac{1}{x-a}\,f(a)+\dfrac{1}{b-x}\,f(b)}{\dfrac{1}{x-a}+\dfrac{1}{b-x}} \qquad\text{with}\qquad \frac{1}{x-a}+\frac{1}{b-x}=\frac{b-a}{(x-a)(b-x)}.

Each data value is now weighted by a function that blows up at its own point. Near a the term 1/(x-a) dominates both numerator and denominator, the ratio tends to “\infty/\infty”, and what survives is f(a). This is the idea of Shepard interpolation, f(x)=\sum_i w_i(x)f(x_i)\,/\sum_j w_j(x) with weights such as w_i=1/\operatorname{dist}(x,x_i).

2 The same trick on a triangle

On a triangle \mathbf p_0\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2 the barycentric coordinates of a point \mathbf x are ratios of areas. Writing A_i for the area of the small triangle spanned by \mathbf x and the side opposite to \mathbf p_i, and A=A_0+A_1+A_2,

f(\mathbf x)=\frac{A_0\,f(\mathbf p_0)+A_1\,f(\mathbf p_1)+A_2\,f(\mathbf p_2)}{A} \;=\;\frac{\dfrac{1}{A_1A_2}f(\mathbf p_0)+\dfrac{1}{A_2A_0}f(\mathbf p_1)+\dfrac{1}{A_0A_1}f(\mathbf p_2)}{\dfrac{A}{A_0A_1A_2}}.

The second expression is obtained by dividing through by A_0A_1A_2. Each area A_i is a linear function of \mathbf x that vanishes on one side of the triangle. So the weight of \mathbf p_0 has poles along the two sides that meet at \mathbf p_0, and the denominator has poles along all three sides.

3 Convex polygons: Wachspress coordinates

For a convex polygon with vertices \mathbf p_0,\dots,\mathbf p_{n-1} one wants generalised barycentric coordinates: functions \lambda_i(\mathbf x)\ge 0 with

\sum_i\lambda_i(\mathbf x)=1\quad\text{(partition of unity)},\qquad \sum_i\lambda_i(\mathbf x)\,\mathbf p_i=\mathbf x\quad\text{(linear precision)}.

For n>3 they are no longer unique. Wachspress found the simplest rational choice. With A_i the area of the triangle \mathbf x\,\mathbf p_i\,\mathbf p_{i+1} and C_i the area of the corner triangle \mathbf p_{i-1}\mathbf p_i\,\mathbf p_{i+1} (indices modulo n),

\lambda_i(\mathbf x)=\frac{w_i(\mathbf x)}{\sum_k w_k(\mathbf x)},\qquad w_i(\mathbf x)=\frac{C_i}{A_{i-1}(\mathbf x)\,A_i(\mathbf x)}.

Once more the weight of a vertex has poles along the two edges through that vertex, and the normalising sum has poles along the whole boundary. You can experiment with these coordinates in Figure 5.

The common pattern. In all three cases numerator and denominator are rational functions with singularities along the “boundaries” of some region, and interpolation happens because they blow up together. Positive geometries make this precise.

4 Positive geometries and canonical forms

Positive geometries were introduced by Arkani-Hamed, Bai and Lam in the study of particle scattering. Informally, a positive geometry is a region X_{\ge0} (a segment, a polygon, a region bounded by lines and conics, …) that comes with a unique canonical form \Omega(X_{\ge0}): a rational differential form that

  • has simple poles along the boundary of the region and no other singularities, and
  • is recursive: its residue along each boundary component is the canonical form of that component, which is again a positive geometry of one dimension less. The recursion ends at points, whose canonical form is \pm1.
Background: differential forms and residues, in two paragraphs

On this page a differential form on a planar region is simply an expression \Omega=C(x,y)\,dx\,dy — a density that could be integrated — and all the information is in the function C, called the canonical function. On a segment the form is C(x)\,dx. We always draw and compute C.

If C blows up like 1/h near a boundary line, where h is the distance to that line, the residue along the line is what remains after removing the pole: \lim_{h\to0}h\,C, a function on the line. It is the several-variable version of the residue of complex analysis.

Background: the precise definition, and a remark on normalisation

A positive geometry is a pair (X,X_{\ge0}), where X is a d-dimensional complex projective variety and X_{\ge0} an oriented semi-algebraic subset of its real part, together with a unique rational d-form \Omega(X,X_{\ge0}) such that: for d=0, X is a point and \Omega=\pm1 according to orientation; for d>0, every boundary component is a positive geometry whose canonical form is the residue of \Omega along it. The same ambient space X carries many positive geometries.

In projective space with homogeneous coordinates the paper writes \Omega=C(\mathbf x)\,\omega(\mathbf x) with a projectively invariant measure \omega. This page works in an affine chart and writes \Omega=C\,dx\,dy. The two normalisations of C differ by a constant factor, which cancels in every ratio below.

The basic examples

Segment. The function met in Section 1 is the canonical function of [a,b]:

\Omega([a,b])=\frac{(b-a)\,dx}{(x-a)(b-x)}=\left(\frac{1}{x-a}+\frac{1}{b-x}\right)dx,

with residues +1 at a and -1 at b (the two end points with opposite orientations).

Triangle. With \langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w\rangle the determinant of the homogeneous coordinates [x:y:1] of three points (twice the signed area of their triangle),

\Omega(\triangle)=\frac{\langle\mathbf p_0,\mathbf p_1,\mathbf p_2\rangle^{2}} {\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf p_0,\mathbf p_1\rangle\,\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf p_1,\mathbf p_2\rangle\,\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf p_2,\mathbf p_0\rangle}\;dx\,dy \;=\;\frac{A^{2}}{2\,A_0A_1A_2}\;dx\,dy ,

which is the denominator of Section 2 up to the constant A/2. For the triangle with vertices (0,0),(1,0),(0,1) this is dx\,dy/\big(xy(1-x-y)\big).

Residues: the boundary of a boundary

The defining property is the recursion. Approach an edge of a polygon and strip off the pole by multiplying with the distance \varepsilon to the edge: in the limit one finds the canonical function of that edge, the segment formula L/\big(s(L-s)\big) for an edge of length L. Taking residues once more, at the two ends of the edge, leaves +1 and -1.

Triangulations and a small zoo

Canonical forms are additive: if a region is cut into pieces, its canonical form is the sum of the forms of the pieces. Along an interior cut the two neighbouring pieces have poles with opposite residues, which cancel. Hence the canonical form of any convex polygon can be found by triangulating it, and convex polytopes are positive geometries.

Generalised simplices — constant numerator:

  • simplices in projective space;
  • regions bounded by a conic and a line (the half disc in Figure 3);
  • positive parts of toric varieties;
  • positive Grassmannians (totally positive matrices).

Generalised polytopes — non-constant numerator:

  • convex polytopes;
  • polycons: convex regions bounded by lines and conics (the pizza slice);
  • Grassmann polytopes, in particular amplituhedra.
Background: why physicists care

In the study of particle scattering, probabilities are computed from rational functions of the particle momenta whose poles correspond to physical particles. For certain theories these functions turn out to be canonical forms of positive geometries such as the amplituhedron: roughly, “probability ≈ volume”. Different ways of organising the calculation correspond to different triangulations of the same shape, in the same way that Section 5 obtains one set of coordinates from different decompositions.

5 Barycentric coordinates as ratios of canonical forms

Here is the proposal of the paper. To get the weight of a vertex, keep only the boundary components that meet at that vertex, close them up with one extra side that depends on the evaluation point, and divide by the canonical form of the whole shape.

Segment. The only boundary component at a is the point a itself. The extra “side” is the point x^* dual to x; in coordinates centred at x it is the point at infinity. So the numerator is the canonical form of the half line [a,\infty], which is dx/(x-a):

\lambda_a(x)=\frac{\Omega([a,x^*])}{\Omega([a,b])} =\frac{1}{x-a}\cdot\frac{(x-a)(b-x)}{b-a}=\frac{b-x}{b-a}.

Triangle and polygon. Let l_{i-1} and l_i be the lines of the two edges through \mathbf p_i, and \mathbf x^* the line dual to \mathbf x. Then

\lambda_i(\mathbf x)=\frac{\Omega\big(\triangle(l_{i-1},\,l_i,\,\mathbf x^*)\big)}{\Omega(P)} .

In the affine picture \mathbf x^* is the line at infinity, so the triangle \triangle(l_{i-1},l_i,\mathbf x^*) is the infinite wedge between the two edge lines. If h_k(\mathbf x) is the distance from \mathbf x to the line l_k and \mathbf n_k its inward unit normal, the canonical function of the wedge is

w_i(\mathbf x)=\frac{\det(\mathbf n_{i-1},\mathbf n_i)}{h_{i-1}(\mathbf x)\,h_i(\mathbf x)}, \qquad\text{and}\qquad \Omega(P)=\sum_i w_i(\mathbf x)\;dx\,dy .

These w_i are exactly the Wachspress weights of Section 3 (up to a common factor 2), so the ratios \lambda_i=w_i/\sum_k w_k are the barycentric coordinates of a triangle and the Wachspress coordinates of a convex polygon.

Why the weights sum to one

The wedges of all vertices overlap in a complicated way, yet their canonical forms add up to \Omega(P): every wedge form has an additional pole along \mathbf x^*, and these poles cancel in the sum. In the language of positive geometries the wedges form a signed triangulation of P, and additivity gives \sum_i\lambda_i=1 without any computation.

The dual picture makes this visible. The canonical function of a convex polytope at \mathbf x is the volume of its polar dual P^*_{\mathbf x}, taken with \mathbf x as origin:

\operatorname{Vol}(P^*_{\mathbf x})=\frac{1}{d!}\int_{\mathbf y\in P^*_{\mathbf x}}\frac{\omega(\mathbf y)}{(\mathbf x^{T}\mathbf y)^{d+1}} \qquad\text{(in the plane: } C_P(\mathbf x)=2\operatorname{Area}(P^*_{\mathbf x})\text{)}.

The wedge at \mathbf p_i corresponds to the triangle \mathbf 0\,\mathbf d_{i-1}\mathbf d_i, and these triangles tile the dual polygon in the ordinary sense. This recovers the description of Wachspress coordinates as ratios of polar dual volumes by Ju, Schaefer, Warren and Desbrun.

One dimension up Added illustration

The paper notes that the generalisation to higher dimensions is straightforward. In d dimensions the recipe applies verbatim to polytopes in which every vertex lies on exactly d facets (simple polytopes; their polar duals are simplicial). For a polyhedron with outward unit face normals \mathbf n_f and distances h_f(\mathbf x) from \mathbf x to the face planes, the wedge at a vertex on the faces a,b,c is the cone bounded by those three planes, and

w_v(\mathbf x)=\frac{|\det(\mathbf n_a,\mathbf n_b,\mathbf n_c)|}{h_a(\mathbf x)\,h_b(\mathbf x)\,h_c(\mathbf x)}, \qquad C_P(\mathbf x)=\sum_v w_v(\mathbf x)=3!\,\operatorname{Vol}(P^*_{\mathbf x}), \qquad \lambda_v=\frac{w_v}{C_P}.

6 The numerator: the adjoint polynomial

Putting the canonical function of an n-gon over a common denominator gives

C_P(\mathbf x)=\frac{\operatorname{adj}_P(\mathbf x)}{h_0(\mathbf x)\,h_1(\mathbf x)\cdots h_{n-1}(\mathbf x)}, \qquad \operatorname{adj}_P(\mathbf x)=\sum_i\det(\mathbf n_{i-1},\mathbf n_i)\!\!\prod_{k\ne i-1,\,i}\!\!h_k(\mathbf x).

The numerator has degree n-3. It is the adjoint polynomial introduced by Wachspress and later defined through triangulations by Warren. Its job is to remove unwanted poles: the denominator vanishes on all edge lines, which also cross each other outside the polygon, but the residue along an edge line may have poles only at the two end points of that edge. So the adjoint curve passes through every intersection point of two non-adjacent edge lines — and this determines it up to a constant factor.

7 Open problems

The framework unifies the adjoint, dual-volume and Shepard-like descriptions of Wachspress coordinates. Its real promise is that positive geometries exist well beyond polytopes. The paper ends with three directions.

Interpolation in line space

The lines of 3D space form the Grassmannian G(2,4), a four-dimensional quadric hypersurface in \mathbb P^5 (Plücker coordinates). Its positive part is a positive geometry, so it has a canonical form with poles along its boundary. A natural analogue of a simplex is bounded by the tetrahedral line complex, built from the lines determined by the vertices of a tetrahedron; its combinatorics is that of an octahedron, the hypersimplex. Can barycentric coordinates for lines be defined as ratios of canonical forms?

Later development While preparing this page (November 2026) we found no published follow-up on barycentric coordinates in line space; as far as we can tell the question is still open.

Mean value coordinates

Mean value coordinates are transcendental, so they cannot be ratios of rational forms. They can, however, be written as Shepard-like interpolants after deforming the boundary into a circle around the current point. A disc is not a positive geometry — it has no zero-dimensional boundary components to end the recursion — but there is still a natural projectively well-defined function singular along a conic \mathbf x^{T}Q\,\mathbf x=0:

\Omega(\mathbb P^2,\mathcal C)=\frac{\pi\,\det(Q)^{3/4}}{(\mathbf x^{T}Q\,\mathbf x)^{3/2}}\;\omega(\mathbf x), \qquad\text{for the unit disc: } C\;\propto\;\frac{1}{(1-x^2-y^2)^{3/2}} .

It is again the dual volume (the polar dual of a disc is an ellipse), but its singularity on the circle is a branch point of order 3/2 and not a simple pole. This suggests looking for generalised positive geometries on branched covers of projective space.

Later development Transforms of regions with curved boundaries that are no longer rational, including exactly this function for the disc, have since been studied; see Section 8.2.

Splines and integral geometry

The dual-volume integral of Section 5 also appears in the theory of multivariate simplex, box and cone splines, as a Laplace transform of indicator functions. Splines and barycentric interpolants might therefore be two faces of one theory based on integral geometry.

Later development The dual-volume and Laplace-transform side of this question has been developed considerably (Section 8.2); we found no work that makes the link to splines explicit.

8 Since the poster Later developments, 2020–2026

Nothing in this section is part of the 2019 paper. It reports work published afterwards that bears directly on its questions. The summaries are ours; please consult the cited papers for the precise statements.

8.1 Curved boundaries: polypols, and the fall of Wachspress’ conjecture

The paper left out regions with curved sides for lack of space. They have since been treated systematically. Kohn, Piene, Ranestad, Rydell, Shapiro, Sinn, Sorea and Telen study polypols: planar regions bounded by arcs of algebraic curves, of which Wachspress’ polycons (lines and conics) are the first case. They prove that every quasi-regular rational polypol is a positive geometry and that its canonical form is, as for polygons,

\Omega(P)=\frac{\operatorname{adj}_P(x,y)}{f_1(x,y)\,f_2(x,y)\cdots f_k(x,y)}\;dx\,dy ,

where f_i=0 are the boundary curves and the numerator is an equation of the adjoint curve, normalised so that the residues at the vertices are \pm1. The adjoint has degree d-3 for a boundary of total degree d and passes through the residual points: the points where boundary curves meet (or are singular) other than the vertices. The pizza slice of Figure 3 is the simplest example: d=4, and the adjoint is the line 1+2y=0.

Wachspress defined rational basis functions on polycons with the adjoint as common denominator. For them to make sense the adjoint must not vanish inside the element, and he conjectured that for a regular polycon it never does. For convex polygons this is true (Figure 7). Kohn et al. classified the 44 topological configurations of three ellipses and proved the conjecture in 33 of them. In March 2026 Clemens Brüser settled the question in the negative: he gave an explicit regular polycon bounded by arcs of three ellipses whose adjoint cubic has an oval in the interior.

What does this mean for interpolation? At a vertex where two lines meet, the wedge recipe of Section 5 still works as it stands: for the corner of the pizza slice it gives \lambda_O=(1-x^2-y^2)/(1+2y), shown in Figure 10. At vertices on a curved side the recipe needs a third “side” to close up a line and a conic, and Wachspress’ own elements add extra nodes on curved sides. A description of those weights as ratios of canonical forms is, to our knowledge, not in the literature. And Brüser’s example shows that positivity of the denominator cannot be taken for granted beyond polygons.

8.2 Dual volumes, Laplace transforms and non-rational forms

The dual-volume formula of Section 5 has been developed in several directions that touch the second and third open problems.

  • Dual mixed volumes. Gao, Lam and Xue (2024) define a dual version of the mixed volume of several polytopes. It specialises to canonical forms and adjoints of polytopes and is additive under mixed subdivisions.
  • Canonical forms as Laplace transforms. Mazzucchelli and Raman (2025) write the canonical function of a positive geometry in projective space as the Laplace transform of a distribution on the convex dual, and call the geometry completely monotone when that distribution is a non-negative measure. Convex polytopes are of this kind. For planar regions bounded by lines and conics they compute the measures explicitly and find transcendental functions (logarithms, or equivalently inverse tangents).
  • Moment-generating functions of polypols. Shapiro (2026) studies the normalised Fantappiè transform of a planar region. For a polygon it is rational: it is the canonical form of the polar polygon. For curved polypols it is in general a branched function built from algebraic functions, logarithms and arctangents. For the unit disc it equals 2\pi\,(1-u^2-v^2)^{-3/2} — algebraic but not rational, and precisely the function plotted for the disc in Figure 9. In agreement with Section 7, that paper does not count the disc among the examples of canonical forms, because a circle has no vertices.

So the non-rational, branched objects that the paper anticipated for mean value coordinates now have a concrete theory around them. Whether mean value coordinates themselves are ratios of such generalised forms has, as far as we know, not been worked out.
